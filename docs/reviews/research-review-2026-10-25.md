# Fact-check of docs/RESEARCH.md (first draft, 2026-10-25)

Reviewer: fact-checker. Document reviewed: `docs/RESEARCH.md`, first
draft dated 2026-10-25. Time spent: about 35 minutes. I did not edit
RESEARCH.md.

## 1. Verdict

**Reliable with corrections.** The core claims the game depends on hold up
against primary texts (SII II nos. 1, 65 and 66; *Song Shi* 489; Hultzsch's
introduction; Kanakasabai p. 130), and the equipment judgments are sound.
The single biggest problem is a pattern: the Thanjavur physical figures in
section 3.1 (240.79 m, 121.92 m, "about 450 m" cloister, 3.81 m stupi,
59.82 m, "1011" for the staff inscription, the 1777 wall) are the English
Wikipedia article's numbers, verbatim, tagged as "[documented]
Balasubrahmanyam 1975; Michell 2008." Balasubrahmanyam, where I could read
him, gives different figures (63.41 m height; stupi "said to be" 3.81 m but
measured at 3.35 m) and the staff inscription is dated to Rajaraja's 29th
year, not 1011. The claims are mostly right; the citations behind them are
not what the tags say. The sword claim is the one substantive error.

Tally over the 15 sampled claims: **11 Confirmed, 2 Mis-tagged, 1 Wrong,
1 Unverifiable** (plus one wrong date, "1011," found inside a confirmed
claim).

## 2. Sample audit

### Claim 1. The 400 temple women

> "Four hundred of them were temple women (talicceri pendugal) housed in
> streets around the temple. [plausible] the figure is standard in the
> literature but I did not read the inscription this session."

What I found: Hultzsch's SII II no. 66 (whatisindia.com transcription,
"no_66_68_outside_of_north_enclosure") lists the women in numbered
paragraphs 3 to 402 with house addresses by street ("the first house of the
southern row of the temple street on the south"). Hultzsch's own summary:
the king had "four hundred women, evidently dancing-girls" transferred.
Men follow in paragraphs 403 to 510. Payment is in shares of one *veli* of
land at 100 *kalam* of paddy per share.

Two corrections. (a) The inscription is dated "until the twenty-ninth year
of Ko-Rajakesarivarman alias Sri-Rajarajadeva," which is 1013–1014 CE, not
"1011" as the timeline row and section 3.1 say. "1011" is Wikipedia's
figure. (b) Hultzsch's translation uses "temple women"; I did not see
"talicceri pendugal" in his text, though the Tamil term is standard in the
later literature (it is the term Sastri and Orr use).

**Result: Confirmed** for the 400 and the streets; the tag can rise to
[documented] SII II no. 66. The "1011" date in the timeline is **Wrong**;
use "c. 1014 (29th regnal year)."

### Claim 2. The 48 hymn singers

> "[plausible] I recall SII II no. 65 appointing 48 singers (pidarar);
> verify."

What I found: SII II no. 65 (whatisindia.com, "no_63_65_outside_of_north
_enclosure"): "forty-eight musicians (Piddarar)" to recite the
Tiruppadiyam, "one person who should constantly beat the small drum" and
"one person who should constantly beat the big drum (kotti-mattalam)," each
at three *kuruni* of paddy a day, fifty persons in all, dated "until the
twenty-ninth year." Hultzsch's introduction identifies the Tiruppadiyam
with the Devaram (Tevaram) hymns.

**Result: Confirmed.** Raise to [documented] SII II no. 65. Note the date is
the 29th year, so these appointments are after the game's 1010.

### Claim 3. Rajaraja's gold kasu: weight, size, design

> "Rajaraja's gold coins of the Lanka occupation are about 4.3 g and 20 mm.
> [documented] coins.lakdiva.org specimen record. The usual design of Chola
> gold and copper of this reign is a standing king on one side and a seated
> figure on the other with a short Nagari legend ... [plausible] from
> standard catalogs I did not open (Mitchiner; Biddulph)."

What I found: coins.lakdiva.org.lk, RajaRaja Chola kahavanu: 4.30 g,
20.2 mm, struck, thickness 2.1 mm. The page does not describe the design.
Spink lot 21117004460 (same type): obverse "standing figure in Ceylon
style, holding a flower in the right hand, with a lamp stand under the
left arm," reverse "figure seated right, holding a conch shell," Devanagari
legend "Sri Raja Raja"; references Codrington 104, Mitchiner *Southern
India* pt. 2, 318. Fran Pritchett's page (quoting vcoins) agrees: seated
figure with legend "Sri / Raja / Raja," standing king with lamp, crescent
and orbs.

**Result: Confirmed.** The design can rise to [documented] with Codrington
104 / Mitchiner 318. One wrinkle: on these specimens the legend sits beside
the *seated* figure, so "no lettering legible at game scale" is the right
instruction for the pickup sprite. The 0.47 gold fineness on the lakdiva
record means these are pale, not bright yellow.

### Claim 4. Chola-period swords were straight, not curved

> "Chola-period swords in bronze and stone are straight, broad,
> double-edged blades with a short grip and a small knob or disc pommel,
> sometimes with a slight flare near the point; the curved talwar is a
> later, northern form. [plausible] from Chola bronzes of Durga, Bhairava
> and Kali and from dvarapala figures."

What I found: Robert Elgood, *Hindu Arms and Ritual* (2015), p. 83, as
quoted on the Ethnographic Arms & Armour forum, on the Gana with a sword in
the Mahishamardini cave at Mamallapuram (mid-7th century, Pallava): "the
forward curved blade which continues in use for centuries," with a square
cushion pommel, and its late form is the *sosun pattah*. Mandarin Mansion's
"Early Indian sword" page, citing Elgood and Elvis: "Swords with a recurved
blade appear in India quite early"; hilts of this type "appear from at
least the Chola dynasty"; and the shift to the talwar comes after Talikota
(1565), replacing "traditional south Indian recurved swords." Kanakasabai
(1956 ed., p. 131, Sangam-era literature) describes swords "broad in the
blade and about a yard long," which supports "broad" but says nothing about
straightness.

So the scholarly picture of early South Indian swords is two forms side by
side: the broad straight *khadga*-type the document describes, and a
forward-curved (kopis-like) blade attested from the 7th century onward. The
document's claim that the period form is straight and curved forms are
later and northern is half right: the back-curved talwar is later and
northern, but a forward-curved South Indian sword is older than the Cholas.

**Result: Wrong**, in the part that matters for the art. The kit's "slightly
curved south-Indian sword" is defensible if the curve is forward (edge on
the convex side, widening toward the tip). The fix is not "make it
straight" but "either a straight broad blade or a forward-curved one; not a
talwar." Retag [plausible] with Elgood 2015, p. 83.

### Claim 5. Guard: crimson lamellar armor

> "'Crimson lamellar armor, rows of lacquered plates over a dark tunic':
> [invented], and the wrong invention. No Chola sculpture, inscription or
> foreign account shows lamellar or lacquered plate in South India ...
> Lacquered lamellar is Tibetan, Chinese and Central Asian."

What I found: nothing that contradicts it. The Wikipedia "Indian armour"
survey, Elgood's Oxford Art Online entry as cited there, and the Met's
Tibetan arms catalog place lamellar in the Himalayan and Central Asian
sphere and riveted mail and plate in India "under the Turks and Mughals."
I found no South Indian lamellar in sculpture or text. One caution the
document should add: its own source Kanakasabai (p. 131) says Sangam-era
generals "wore coats of mail" and arsenals held "breast-plates," so "no
armor at all" overstates; armor for elite figures is attested in literature
eight centuries earlier, just not lamellar and not for a gate guard.

**Result: Confirmed** as a negative claim (no counter-evidence found); keep
[invented] for the lamellar, and the "quilted cotton jacket" alternative
stays [plausible].

### Claim 6. Guard: conical bronze helmet with red plume

> "'Conical bronze helmet with a red plume': [invented]. No helmet is
> attested. Dvarapalas wear crowns; soldiers in relief wear a cloth head
> band or turban or a bare topknot."

What I found: no helmet in any Chola-period South Indian source I reached.
The Indian-armour survey's earliest helmet references are Vedic textual
("shirastrana") and the later Deccan helmets are 17th century. Kanakasabai's
Sangam synthesis mentions no helmets. Dvarapalas at Thanjavur wear
*kirita* crowns, as the document says.

**Result: Confirmed** (negative claim; no counter-evidence). [invented] is
the right tag.

### Claim 7. Guard: round dark-wood shield

> "'Round dark-wood shield slung on the back': [plausible]. Round and
> oblong hide-and-wood shields appear in Tamil literature (bucklers of
> ox-hide, Kanakasabai 1904, p. 130, as reported; not opened) and on hero
> stones."

What I found: I downloaded the 1956 reprint of Kanakasabai from
archive.org. Page 130–131, in "Social Life": "The ordinary foot soldiers
carried in their left hand large bucklers made of ox-hide and in their
right hand a lance or a battle-axe ... The cavalry carried lighter arms and
shorter bucklers than those supplied to the infantry ... They [generals]
carried lances or swords, and shields adorned with gold and wore coats of
mail." His footnote cites *Silappadikaram* XV. The page number holds.

Two notes. Kanakasabai is describing the Sangam age (he dates it to the
first centuries CE), 800 years before 1010; the document should say
"Sangam-era literature" rather than imply Chola evidence. And the citation
"as reported" matches, word for word, a post on the ponniyinselvan.in fan
forum ("Kanakasabai, page 130, refers to Bucklers made of Ox-Hide"); the
historian should cite the book now that it is confirmed, not the forum.

**Result: Confirmed.** [plausible] is right. Ox-hide, not "dark wood," is
the attested material; the document already says so.

### Claim 8. Guard: long spear with leaf-shaped iron head

> "'Long spear with a leaf-shaped iron head': [plausible]. The vel is the
> standard Tamil weapon from Sangam poetry onward; a leaf blade is the
> common form in sculpture."

What I found: Kanakasabai p. 130 has foot soldiers with "a lance or a
battle-axe" and lancers as a class; Zhao Rugua on Zhunian (via the Chola
military summary) says they "fight with spears at close quarters." The leaf
blade shape I could not test against a specific published sculpture in the
time available, but it is the form of the vel in Murugan iconography of the
period.

**Result: Confirmed.** [plausible] is right.

### Claim 9. Hero: crimson silk sash

> "'Crimson silk sash': silk was imported and costly; a soldier's sash is
> cotton. [plausible] Keep the color, drop 'silk.'"

What I found: no source checked gives a Chola soldier's sash material, and
the document claims none. The *Song Shi* 489 entry for the 1015 mission
lists pearl garments and a pearl cap among the gifts and says nothing of
silk going either way; Chinese silk was a Chola-side import in this period,
and the Kanchipuram silk tradition is documented much later. The inference
is sound.

**Result: Confirmed** as a [plausible] inference with no source claimed.

### Claim 10. Vimana height, tiers, base, finial

> "The vimana stands on a 30.18 m square base and rises in 13 tapering
> tiers to an octagonal cupola; published heights range from 59.8 m to
> 65.8 m, so the game's 'sixty-six metres' sits at the top of the range and
> should read 'about sixty-five.' [documented] Balasubrahmanyam 1975,
> pp. 25–36. It is granite throughout, with a single monolithic capping
> block and a gilded copper finial (stupi) 3.81 m tall. [documented] same."

What I found: Balasubrahmanyam's Thanjavur chapter (wisdomlib transcription
of *Middle Chola Temples*): the garbhagriha "measures 30.18 ms by 30.18 ms
at the base," "thirteen storeys (talas)," and "rises to a grand height of
63.41 ms from the floor of the inner courtyard" (Fergusson's older figure
57.91 m is quoted). Capstone "a single block of granite 7.77 ms square
estimated to weigh about eighty tons." The stupi "is said to be about
3.81 ms in height," but recent cleaning measured the kalasam at "only
3.35 ms." UNESCO's nomination file: "the 65 m tall sanctum tower," 13
tiers, 30 x 30 m. Wikipedia gives both 63.4 m and 59.82 m.

So the 30.18 m, 13 tiers and monolithic cap are confirmed in
Balasubrahmanyam. The "range 59.8 to 65.8" is not his; he gives 63.41 m.
The range is real across the literature (Wikipedia 59.82, Balasubrahmanyam
63.41, UNESCO 65), so "about sixty-five" or "over sixty metres" are both
safe. The stupi "3.81 m [documented] same" is Mis-tagged: Balasubrahmanyam
reports 3.81 only as "said to be," against a measured 3.35 m.

**Result: Mis-tagged.** Height range: [plausible], "published figures vary
from 59.8 m (ASI/Wikipedia) to 65 m (UNESCO); Balasubrahmanyam gives
63.41 m." Stupi: [plausible] "3.35–3.81 m (Balasubrahmanyam 1975)." Base,
tiers and cap stay [documented].

### Claim 11. Kalasam date: 275th day of the 25th year

> "1010 | 275th day of Rajaraja's 25th year: he presents the gold-plated
> kalasam for the vimana's finial | [documented] Thanjavur inscription,
> SII II (Balasubrahmanyam 1975)"

What I found: SII II no. 1, paragraph 18 (whatisindia.com): in "the 25th
year of his reign, on the 275th day," the king "gave one copper water-pot
(kuta), to be placed on the copper pinnacle (stupittari)," 3,083 *pala* of
copper, over which gold plates (*tagadu*) weighing 2,926½ *karanju* "were
laid." Balasubrahmanyam cites the same ("SII, II, 1, para 18"). Regnal year
25 of a 985 accession is 1009–1010.

**Result: Confirmed.** Add the inscription number (no. 1, para 18). The
inscription calls it a copper *kuta* with gold plates, which the document's
"gold-plated kalasam" renders fairly.

### Claim 12. The 1015 embassy: 21,100 liang of pearls and 60 tusks

> "1015 | Chola embassy reaches the Song court with 21,100 liang of pearls
> and 60 elephant tusks | [documented] *Song Shi*, via Tansen Sen, 'Chinese
> Sources on the Cholas'"

What I found: *Song Shi* juan 489, Zhunian section (zh.wikisource.org), for
大中祥符八年 (1015): envoy 娑里三文 with a vice-envoy, a judge and an escort
officer, 52 persons in all; the memorial lists "真珠衫帽各一、真珠二萬一千一百兩、象牙六十株、乳香六十斤"
(one pearl garment and one pearl cap, 21,100 liang of pearls, 60 elephant
tusks, 60 jin of frankincense), plus a separate presentation of 6,600 liang
of pearls and 3,300 jin of aromatics. The academia.edu copy of Sen's paper
would not open beyond its abstract, so I verified against the primary text
instead.

**Result: Confirmed**, and the citation can be made primary: *Song Shi*
489. Two bonuses for the historian: the same juan records the 1033 mission
(明道二年, envoy 蒲押陁離, "真珠一百五兩、象牙百株"), so the timeline's
"[plausible] ... not read by me" row can become [documented]; and the 1028
Srivijaya embassy (天聖六年) is there too, with no quantities.

### Claim 13. Rajendra was about 39 in 1010

> "He was about 39, already a general in Lanka and the Deccan, and two years
> from formal co-regency ... [documented] birth c. 971 (Kramrisch 1975,
> p. 306, flagged as speculative); co-regency 1012 (Sastri 1955)."

What I found: no primary source gives Rajendra's birth year. The Wikipedia
article says outright "The exact birth date of Rajendra I is unknown; it is
speculated he was born around 971 CE," while its infobox prints "26 July
971," a date with no inscriptional basis that I could find. Co-regency in
1012 and sole rule from 1014 are standard (Sastri) and I accept them. That
Rajendra had already commanded in the field before 1012 is consistent with
the Tiruvalangadu plates' account, but the document does not cite it.

The claim's point for the game is sound: a man who became co-regent in 1012
and ruled 30 years after that was not a "young prince" in 1010. But "about
39" rests on a speculative birth year, and the document itself calls the
source speculative while tagging the sentence [documented].

**Result: Mis-tagged.** Birth year and age: [plausible]. "Two years from
co-regency": [documented] Sastri. Suggested wording: "a grown man, already
a commander, two years from co-regency; his birth year is not recorded."

### Claim 14. Enclosure 240.79 m by 121.92 m; cloister about 450 m

> "The temple enclosure is a walled rectangle 240.79 m east–west by
> 121.92 m north–south ... [documented] Balasubrahmanyam 1975; Michell
> 2008, pp. 89–91 ... A pillared cloister (prakara) about 450 m long runs
> around the inside of the wall ... [documented] same."

What I found: UNESCO's nomination document gives "ca 240 m east-west and
120 m north-south." Wikipedia gives "240.79 metres (790.0 ft)" and "121.92
metres (400.0 ft)," which are 790 and 400 feet converted, and "a perimeter
of about 450 metres (1,480 ft) for circumambulation." Balasubrahmanyam's
online chapter gives no enclosure dimensions; the 54.86 by 30.18 figure
there is the mandapa-plus-sanctum footprint. I could not open Michell.

The enclosure figures are confirmed in substance (UNESCO), but the exact
decimals and the "450 m" are Wikipedia's, not Balasubrahmanyam's or
Michell's as tagged. The 450 m also fails arithmetic: a cloister lining the
inside of a 240 by 122 m wall is about 700 m around. Wikipedia's "perimeter
of about 450 m" may refer to a different circuit (the inner platform) or be
an error; either way it does not describe the cloister.

**Result: Unverifiable** for the 450 m cloister length; **Confirmed** for
the enclosure at "about 240 by 120 m" with the tag changed to UNESCO 2004
nomination file (or to Michell once opened). Drop the two-decimal
precision.

### Claim 15. The regiments, and the gate guards

> "The Thanjavur inscriptions name regiments by royal title:
> Keralantaka-vasal-tirumeykappar and Anukka-vasal-tirumeykappar (bodyguards
> of the Keralantaka gate and the inner gate), Pandita-Chola-terinda-villigal
> (archers), Nigarili-Chola-terinda-udanilai-kudiraicchevagar (cavalry),
> Mummudi-Chola-terinda-anaippagar (elephant corps), and many 'terinda
> kaikkolar' infantry units named for kings. [documented] Sastri 1935,
> p. 200."

What I found: Hultzsch's introduction to SII II (whatisindia.com) lists 31
regiments named as donors in the Tanjore inscriptions, including
"Keralantaka-vasal-tirumeykappar," "Anukka-vasal-tirumeykappar,"
"Pandita-Sora-terinda-villigal," "Nigarili-Sora-terinda-Udanilai-
Kudiraichchevagar" and "Mummadi-Sora-terinda-Anaippagar." Hultzsch's
footnote to no. 57 glosses *tiru-mey-kappu* as "the sacred body-guard" and
says the *mey-kappar* were in three divisions, two guarding the Keralantaka
gate and the Anukka gate. Thirteen of the 31 are Valangai-Velaikkara units.
Hultzsch does not translate the regiment names; the glosses "archers,"
"cavalry," "elephant corps" are Sastri's and are the natural readings of
*villigal*, *kudiraichchevagar* and *anaippagar*.

Two small things. "Terinda kaikkolar" is not in Hultzsch's Tanjore list;
the Tanjore units are *terinda-parivarattar* and *terinda-Valangai-
Velaikkarar*, and kaikkolar units appear in other Chola inscriptions. And
the spelling is Mummadi (Hultzsch) or Mummudi (Sastri); either is fine.

**Result: Confirmed.** The gate-guard identification the writer is offered
("tirumeykappar of the Keralantaka gate") holds and can be cited to SII II,
Hultzsch's introduction and no. 57 n. 2, a primary-edition source, rather
than Sastri at second hand.

## 3. Missing

Checked against the level text in `index.html` and the prompts in
`ASSETS.md`.

- **Title screen: "Emperor Rajendra's expedition."** The document covers
  "Ganges-conqueror, lord of the eastern sea" but not whether "Emperor" is
  acceptable. Chola kings were *perumal* / *udaiyar* / *ko*; "emperor" is a
  modern convenience. One line would settle it.
- **End screen: "Trade with Song China flowed free, and Tamil merchant
  guilds rose across Southeast Asia."** Section 3.3 handles "only great
  overseas expedition" and "Kadaram has fallen" but says nothing about
  whether trade with China "flowed free" after 1025 (the 1033 and 1077
  missions are the evidence, and the *Song Shi* 489 entry supports a 1033
  mission). Also "Tamil merchant guilds rose": the document says the guilds
  were pan-South-Indian, not Tamil; the end screen contradicts section 7
  and needs a flagged fix.
- **End screen title: "The Sea Belongs to the Tiger."** The tiger as Chola
  emblem is assumed everywhere in the game (tiger banner, "tiger" title)
  but the document only mentions the tiger on older coins. One sentence on
  the tiger crest (the *puli* on the Chola flag and seal) with a source
  would protect the game's most repeated image.
- **Level 1 text: "Cross the temple grounds by the near and far paths."**
  The game's two lanes are the mechanic; the document does not say what
  the two circuits of the temple would be (outer court and cloister
  gallery) or whether the cloister's upper storey existed in 1010.
  Balasubrahmanyam describes a two-storied prakara; the artist needs to
  know if it is period.
- **Lamplit gates (gate.png): "a small South Indian torana gateway ... two
  granite pillars with carved lotus bands, supporting a single curved stone
  lintel."** The document gives the torana of Srivijaya (section 3.3) but
  never says whether a free-standing stone torana is a Chola form at all.
  It is not typical of Tamil temple architecture (the Chola gate is the
  gopura); a one-line verdict is needed, since the prop appears in all
  three levels.
- **Oil lamps in gate niches.** Covered in general ("oil lamps
  everywhere") but the brass lamp type (*kuthuvilakku* standing lamp vs.
  wall niche) is not, and the prompt asks for a niche lamp.
- **"Monsoon jungle" and "ravine ... rope bridge" in level 3.** The
  document describes Kedah as mangrove coast and river plain; "ravine" and
  "steep jungle ridges" (l3_far.png) are not addressed. Gunung Jerai is
  mentioned, which gives the artist the one hill that is defensible.
- **The hero's sandals.** "Sandals [plausible]; bare feet are likelier on
  deck" is in the document, but ASSETS.md asks for "simple leather
  sandals" in all five hero prompts and nobody is told whether to change
  it. A decision line is needed.
- **Coins as pickups: "+1 kasu."** Covered for the word and the coin. Not
  covered: whether a gold kasu is the right denomination for loose
  pickups (gold was high value; copper kasu existed). Small point, but the
  game shows a dozen on every level.
- **Guard behavior.** Not the document's job, but the level 2 and 3
  guards are the same "Chola imperial guard" sprite. In level 3 the enemies
  should be Srivijayan; the document's "People and dress" for Malays gives
  the artist what is needed but does not say the guard sprite must change.
  Flag it for the art director in NOTES.md.

## 4. Bias check

The Srivijaya section does give the other side, and does it well: it names
the king, cites Old Malay inscriptions, notes the 1028 embassy, says the
kingdom survived, and refuses to let the game stage a religious war. "Write
Rajendra's boast as a boast" is the right instruction. Section 7's "Modern
nationalism" entry addresses the "Tamil conquest of Southeast Asia" framing
directly.

Two residual issues:

- The document says the Chola sources are "silent" on the fate of the
  captured king and city, and treats that silence as [documented]. Silence
  is not documentation; it should read "[plausible] the inscription says
  nothing beyond the list." This is fair to Srivijaya, but the tag is wrong
  in the same direction as the others.
- Kanakasabai (1904) is a colonial-era work that reads Sangam poetry as
  straight reportage of a Tamil golden age; the document uses him for
  shields and sword vocabulary without saying so. He is fine as a pointer
  to the poems, not as a witness. The document's own "Ponniyin Selvan as
  history" rule should extend to "Kanakasabai as history."

I found no colonial-era framing in the document's own prose. It does not
use "Hindu colonization" language, and the "thalassocracy" label for
Srivijaya is standard.
