## 1. How to read this

Every factual sentence carries a tag. **[documented]** means a primary source
(inscription, contemporary text, excavation) or a peer-reviewed secondary
source that cites one, named inline. **[plausible]** means consistent with
the evidence but not directly attested, with the inference stated.
**[invented]** means history is silent and the game must choose; I say what
is least wrong. Where I cite a book I could not open this session, I say so
in section 8. Best general reads: K. A. Nilakanta Sastri, *The Cōḷas* (1935,
2nd ed. 1955); Kulke, Kesavapany and Sakhuja (eds.), *Nagapattinam to
Suvarnadwipa* (ISEAS, 2009); George Michell, *Architecture and Art of
Southern India* (1995/2008).

## 3. The three settings

### 3.1 Thanjavur, c. 1010

**Physical environment.** The temple enclosure is a walled rectangle
240.79 m east–west by 121.92 m north–south, entered from the east through two
gateways in line: the outer Keralantakan tiruvasal and the inner Rajarajan
tiruvasal, both barrel-vaulted and both well under half the height of the
vimana. [documented] Balasubrahmanyam 1975; Michell 2008, pp. 89–91. The
vimana stands on a 30.18 m square base and rises in 13 tapering tiers to an
octagonal cupola; published heights range from 59.8 m to 65.8 m, so the
game's "sixty-six metres" sits at the top of the range and should read
"about sixty-five." [documented] Balasubrahmanyam 1975, pp. 25–36. It is
granite throughout, with a single monolithic capping block and a gilded
copper finial (stupi) 3.81 m tall. [documented] same. A pillared cloister
(prakara) about 450 m long runs around the inside of the wall; a monolithic
Nandi faces the sanctum from a pavilion on the axis. [documented] same. The
original moat has been filled; the outer fort wall is a 1777 addition and
must not appear. [documented] Michell 2008.

In 1010 the stone was new, unstained, and the gopurams were lower than any
Vijayanagara or Nayak gopuram the artist has seen. [plausible] from the
dated fabric; the tall later gopurams of Madurai and Srirangam are
16th–17th century. Whether the vimana was plastered and painted white or
left bare granite is not settled; traces of lime plaster and later paint
exist, but the 1010 surface is unknown. [plausible] Do not add red lacquer
to exterior carvings as the stone texture prompt asks; keep "faint red" for
interior murals only. The painted murals in the sanctum ambulatory, about
670 sq m, are Chola work and mostly Shaiva, with one royal scene usually
read as Rajaraja with his guru. [documented] Sriraman 2010, pp. 167–172.

**People.** The 1011 inscription names more than 600 people on the payroll:
priests, lamp-lighters, washermen, tailors, jewelers, potters, carpenters,
parasol bearers, dance teachers, dancers, singers, drummers, accountants
and superintendents, each paid in shares of village land. [documented] SII
II no. 66 (Balasubrahmanyam 1975). Four hundred of them were temple women
(talicceri pendugal) housed in streets around the temple. [plausible] the
figure is standard in the literature but I did not read the inscription
this session. Soldiers of named regiments made gifts to the temple and
stood guard at its gates; see section 4. [documented] Sastri 1935, p. 200.

**Who is Rajendra in 1010.** He was about 39, already a general in Lanka and
the Deccan, and two years from formal co-regency. The level text's "young
prince Rajendra" is wrong. [documented] birth c. 971 (Kramrisch 1975,
p. 306, flagged as speculative); co-regency 1012 (Sastri 1955). Fix: "the
prince Rajendra" or "Rajendra, the king's son and general."

**Sounds and objects.** Oil lamps everywhere: the inscriptions record gifts
of sheep and cows whose ghee fed perpetual lamps. [documented] SII II
(lamp-lighters in no. 66; livestock-for-lamps grants are the commonest Chola
temple record). Conch, drums, cymbals, and Tevaram hymn-singing.
[plausible] I recall SII II no. 65 appointing 48 singers (pidarar); verify.
Parasols, fly-whisks, garlands, brass vessels, sandal paste, camphor flame.
[plausible] from the personnel list and Shaiva ritual.

**What the game gets wrong here.** (a) "Sixty-six metres." (b) "Young
prince." (c) The backdrop's "many tiered gopuram towers": there are two,
both low. (d) Crimson banners on the temple walls: the attested flag is the
kodi on a flagstaff; long streamers off the walls are [invented]. Keep one
or two on poles. (e) "Granite vimana rises over the city" needs no change.

### 3.2 Nagapattinam, c. 1015–1025

**The port.** Nagapattinam was the Chola kingdom's main eastern harbor and
the entry point for traders and envoys from Sri Lanka, Burma, the Malay
peninsula, Sumatra and Java. [documented] Seshadri in Kulke 2009, ch. 7;
Fontein 1980. Chinese ceramic sherds of Song date found along the Coromandel
coast and in Sri Lanka show the China trade running through these ports.
[documented] Karashima in Kulke 2009, ch. 2. The town sits on a flat sandy
coast with the Kaveri delta behind it: no cliffs, no hills, river mouths,
lagoons and backwaters. [plausible] from the geography; the medieval
shoreline is not mapped.

**The vihara.** The Chudamani vihara was begun by the Srivijayan
(Sailendra) king Chudamanivarman and completed by his son Mara
Vijayottungavarman, with Rajaraja's consent and his grant of the village
Anaimangalam in 1006. [documented] Larger Leiden plates (Mahalingam 1948;
Goh and Miksic 2016). It was Buddhist, foreign-funded, and in a Shaiva
king's chief port; the game should show it. More than 350 Buddhist bronzes
dated 11th to 16th century have been found at Nagapattinam, many with a
flame on the ushnisha. [documented] Fontein 1980; Chennai Museum. A tall
brick tower of the complex stood until Jesuits demolished it in 1867.
[documented] Mahalingam 1948. Its height and plan are not recorded in any
source I reached. [plausible] A tall square brick tower, plastered, with a
tiered top, is the least wrong rendering.

**Traders and guilds.** The level text names "the Five Hundred Lords of
Ayyavole." The guild existed and operated overseas: its Tamil inscription of
1088 at Lobu Tua near Barus in Sumatra records the "Five Hundred of the
thousand directions" meeting at the velapuram there. [documented] Lobu Tua
inscription (Christie 1998). Ayyavole is Aihole in Karnataka; the guild was
pan-South-Indian, not Chola or Tamil by origin. [documented] same. Whether
the Five Hundred had a presence at Nagapattinam in 1015–1025 is not
attested; the Manigramam and Nanadesi guilds and the local nagaram
merchants are the safer names. [plausible] from the ninth-century Takuapa
inscription (Manikkiramam) and Champakalakshmi's work on nagaram towns.
Foreigners in port would include Sinhalese, Malays and Sumatrans, Burmese
monks, Arab and Persian merchants (the Anjuvannam guild of West Asian
traders is attested on the west coast), and Chinese crews. [plausible]
Subbarayalu, "Anjuvannam," in Kulke 2009, ch. 9; Chinese crews are
inferred from the ceramics.

**Cargo.** The 1015 embassy carried pearls and elephant tusks to China,
which is the best list of what the Cholas sent east. [documented] *Song
Shi* via Sen. Cotton textiles were a Chola export to Southeast Asia.
[documented] Devare in Kulke 2009, ch. 11 (chapter title only; not read).
Inbound: Chinese ceramics, camphor and benzoin from Barus, aromatics, tin.
[plausible] from the Lobu Tua context and Zhao Rugua. War elephants loaded
for export, as the level text says, are not attested; the Cholas captured
elephants in Kadaram rather than shipping them out. [invented] Replace
with "pearls, cloth and rice."

**Ships.** Indian Ocean hulls of this period were sewn-plank: planks
stitched edge to edge with coir cord through drilled holes, caulked, with
no iron nails. [documented] Staples and Blue 2019, pp. 269–285; Marco Polo
and Ibn Battuta both remark on the nail-less ships of the Malabar coast.
Southeast Asian hulls used the related stitched-plank and lashed-lug
method, with multiple masts, canted rectangular sails and sometimes
outriggers, as on the Borobudur ship reliefs (c. 800). [plausible]
Manguin's work (not opened). The `tex_wood.png` prompt's "iron nail heads"
are wrong; ask for "stitched seams of dark coir cord in a zigzag along the
plank edges, wooden dowels." The `l2_far.png` prompt's "lateen-style sails"
belong to Arab dhows; Indian and Malay sails were square or canted
rectangles of matting or cotton. [plausible] Sakhuja and Sakhuja in Kulke
2009, ch. 4, argue the fleet was requisitioned merchant shipping;
Subbarayalu, ch. 5, finds no evidence of a standing Chola navy.
[documented] as reported in the volume summary; chapters not read. The
ship names "kalamukha," "manthai" and "sandhani" that circulate online
come from Sakhuja, whose source is not a Chola text. [plausible] Keep them
off the sources screen.

**A dock scene an artist can draw.** Low flat coast, late afternoon. Broad
beamy wooden hulls drawn up on sand or anchored in the river mouth, planks
visibly stitched, one or two masts with yards of matting sail furled, no
iron fittings, no stern castles. Catamarans and dugouts moving cargo.
Thatched sheds and lime-plastered brick warehouses; bales of cotton cloth,
baskets of rice, jars, areca bundles, stacked ceramic bowls, pepper sacks.
Bare-chested Tamil porters in short dhotis and head cloths; a merchant in a
white dhoti and shoulder cloth with gold earrings; a shaven monk in ochre;
a Malay trader in a wrapped waist cloth; a bearded Arab in a long robe; a
Chinese sailor in jacket and trousers. A brick Buddhist tower behind the
sheds; palmyra and coconut palms. All [plausible] except the sewn hulls and
the vihara, [documented] above.

### 3.3 Srivijaya and Kadaram, 1025

**What the raid was.** Rajendra's Thanjavur inscription says he sent "many
ships in the midst of the rolling sea," captured Sangrama Vijayottungavarman
"the king of Kadaram" with his elephants and treasure, seized the "jeweled
wicket-gate" (vidyadhara-torana) at the war gate of Srivijaya, and took
twelve more places: Pannai, Malaiyur, Mayirudingam, Ilangasoka, Mapappalam,
Mevilimbangam, Valaippanduru, Talaittakkolam, Madamalingam, Ilamuridesam,
Manakkavaram and Kadaram itself. [documented] Thanjavur inscription of
Rajendra; Tiruvalangadu plates, 14th regnal year (Kulke 2009, pp. 1–19;
Sastri 1955, pp. 211–220). Identifications: Srivijaya is Palembang;
Kadaram is Kedah; Pannai is Panai in north Sumatra; Malaiyur is Jambi;
Ilangasoka is Langkasuka; Talaittakkolam is Takuapa or Trang; Manakkavaram
is the Nicobars; Mapappalam is in lower Burma. [documented] Coedes 1968;
Sastri 1955, pp. 211–220; several remain disputed. It was a raid, not a
conquest: no Chola administration followed, and the Cholas may have taken
periodic tribute. [documented] Kulke 2009; Spencer 1983. The motive is
unsettled: Sastri proposed Srivijayan interference with Chola trade or
Rajendra's wish to extend his conquests overseas; Spencer called it the
"politics of plunder"; Sen allows a trade war over access to Song China.
[documented] Kulke 2009, pp. 1–19. Fleet size, route, and how long the
crossing took are not recorded anywhere. [invented] Any number the game
gives is invented; "many ships" is the only honest phrase.

**The Srivijayan side.** Srivijaya was a Malay Buddhist thalassocracy
centered on Palembang on the Musi river, with Jambi, Kedah and the
peninsular ports as members of its network. [documented] Yijing's
seventh-century account; Coedes 1968. Chinese sources describe a people
living partly on rafts and houses on the water, skilled fighters on land
and sea who supplied their own gear, and a port that forced passing ships
to call and attacked those that did not. [documented] Zhao Rugua, *Zhufan
zhi* (1225), via Sen in Kulke 2009 and the *Sanfotsi* summary; the text is
two centuries later than the raid. Srivijaya's kings corresponded with
Song China, had funded a monastery in Rajaraja's own port, and sent an
embassy to China in 1028, three years after the raid. [documented] *Song
Shi*; Larger Leiden plates. The kingdom survived; its center drifted to
Jambi, and Virarajendra raided Kedah again in 1068. [documented] Kulke
2009; Sastri 1955. Nothing says the captured king was killed or Palembang
destroyed. [documented] silence of the Thanjavur inscription beyond the
list. The end screen's "Kadaram has fallen" is a Chola boast; label it as
Ilamaran's belief or add the cost. Its "only great overseas naval
expedition in Indian history" is false: the Chola campaigns in Sri Lanka
(993, 1017) and the 1068 Kedah raid were also overseas. [documented] Sastri
1955. Fix: "the largest overseas expedition any Indian ruler is known to
have sent."

**Landscape and architecture.** Kedah's port of this period is at Sungai
Mas (5th–11th century, mostly Buddhist finds) and, from the 11th century,
Pengkalan Bujang (mainly Hindu finds) on the Merbok estuary: a mangrove
coast, a low river plain, and Gunung Jerai rising behind. [documented]
Bujang Valley survey (Jacq-Hergoualc'h 1992; Quaritch Wales 1940).
Shrines there are small brick and laterite candi, a few meters on a side,
on stepped plinths; Candi Bukit Batu Pahat is the best known. [documented]
same. Palembang's own monuments are brick, and the early city was largely
wood and thatch on stilts and on the river. [documented] Zhao Rugua;
Palembang archaeology at Bukit Seguntang (Coedes 1968). The `l3_far.png`
prompt's "terraced Buddhist stupa sanctuaries in the manner of Borobudur"
is wrong by place and style: Borobudur is in central Java, a different
kingdom, and nothing like it stood in Kedah or Sumatra. [documented] Fix:
"low square brick shrines with stepped plinths and a tall tiered brick
tower, plastered white, among stilt houses on a mangrove river." The
level's "stupa terraces" platform set can stay as a ruined brick plinth.
The "citadel of the maharaja" is [invented]: no fortress is attested; a
timber palisade with a carved gateway (the inscription's own torana) is
the least wrong image, and it gives the game a reason for its gate prop.

**People and dress.** Malay men in a wrapped waist cloth to the knee, bare
chest, a head cloth; chiefs with gold. [plausible] The kris is not securely
attested before the 13th century; prefer a straight short sword or spear.
[plausible] Buddhist monks in robes; Sanskrit and Old Malay on stone.
[documented] Old Malay inscriptions of the 680s (Kedukan Bukit, Talang
Tuwo). Sounds: river water, bronze gongs, cicadas, rain. [plausible]

## 4. People and roles

**Rajaraja I** (r. 985–1014), born Arulmolivarman, titles Mummudi Cholan,
Keralantakan, Sivapadasekharan. His inscriptions open with the Tamil
eulogy "Tirumagal pola." [documented] Sastri 1955. **Rajendra I** (co-regent
1012, r. 1014–1044), titles Gangaikonda Cholan, Kadaram-kondan ("who took
Kadaram"), Mudigonda Cholan; eulogy "Tirumanni valara." [documented] Sastri
1955, p. 228 for family. The level text's "Ganges-conqueror, lord of the
eastern sea" renders Gangaikonda fairly; "lord of the eastern sea" is a
paraphrase, not a title. [plausible]

**Army organization.** The Thanjavur inscriptions name regiments by royal
title: Keralantaka-vasal-tirumeykappar and Anukka-vasal-tirumeykappar
(bodyguards of the Keralantaka gate and the inner gate), Pandita-Chola-
terinda-villigal (archers), Nigarili-Chola-terinda-udanilai-kudiraicchevagar
(cavalry), Mummudi-Chola-terinda-anaippagar (elephant corps), and many
"terinda kaikkolar" infantry units named for kings. [documented] Sastri
1935, p. 200. A gift to the writer: the guards at level 1's temple gate
are the "tirumeykappar of the Keralantaka gate." Commanders held titles
such as senapati, nayakan and dandanayakan, with honorifics velan or
araiyan. [plausible] Subbarayalu (not opened). "Velaikkarar" were the
king's sworn troops; the term is secure, the "suicide corps" reading is
not. [documented] Sastri 1955; Stein. A common soldier was a "sevagan."
[plausible]

**The hero.** "Warrior of the Chola navy" presumes a navy; the evidence is
for ships and soldiers put on them. [documented] Subbarayalu in Kulke
2009, ch. 5. Better: "a soldier of the fleet." The name Ilamaran ("Ilam,"
young, plus "Maran," a Pandya royal name) reads well but I cannot attest it
for a Chola soldier. [plausible] Attested Chola-period names run "X son of
Y of village Z": Kandan, Adittan, Solan, Udaiyan, Sattan, Kuttan. "Kandan"
is secure if a change is wanted; otherwise Ilamaran is a free invention
that VISION.md permits. His kit dress (bare chest, dhoti tucked kachcham
style, cross-belt, topknot, earrings, armlets, bangles) matches Chola
bronze and relief conventions; the cross-belt is the channavira of
sculpture. [plausible] from sculpture, which shows gods and donors, not
sailors. Sandals [plausible]; bare feet are likelier on deck. "Crimson silk
sash": silk was imported and costly; a soldier's sash is cotton.
[plausible] Keep the color, drop "silk."

**The guard.** Tested item by item.
- "Crimson lamellar armor, rows of lacquered plates over a dark tunic":
  [invented], and the wrong invention. No Chola sculpture, inscription or
  foreign account shows lamellar or lacquered plate in South India; the
  figures in Chola reliefs fight bare-chested. Lacquered lamellar is Tibetan,
  Chinese and Central Asian. Least wrong: bare chest with a crossed leather
  or cloth belt and a crimson cloth wrapped around the torso, or, if the
  artist needs armor for silhouette, a quilted cotton jacket (attested
  later in South India, [plausible] for the period).
- "Conical bronze helmet with a red plume": [invented]. No helmet is
  attested. Dvarapalas wear crowns; soldiers in relief wear a cloth head
  band or turban or a bare topknot. Least wrong: a crimson head cloth, or
  a plain iron skullcap if a hard head is needed for readability.
- "Round dark-wood shield slung on the back": [plausible]. Round and oblong
  hide-and-wood shields appear in Tamil literature (bucklers of ox-hide,
  Kanakasabai 1904, p. 130, as reported; not opened) and on hero stones.
  Make it hide over wicker or wood, with a boss.
- "Long spear with a leaf-shaped iron head": [plausible]. The vel is the
  standard Tamil weapon from Sangam poetry onward; a leaf blade is the
  common form in sculpture.
- "Chola imperial guard": [documented] the tirumeykappar regiments above.
  "Imperial" is a modern word; "royal bodyguard" or "gate guard."

## 5. Weapons and combat

Tamil sources from the Sangam anthologies on give a stable vocabulary: vel
(spear), val (sword), vil and ambu (bow and arrow), kedayam or kidugu
(shield), and the war drum. [documented] Purananuru and Patirruppattu as
surveyed in Sastri 1955 and Kanakasabai 1904; I did not read the poems this
session. Chola-period swords in bronze and stone are straight, broad,
double-edged blades with a short grip and a small knob or disc pommel,
sometimes with a slight flare near the point; the curved talwar is a later,
northern form. [plausible] from Chola bronzes of Durga, Bhairava and
Kali and from dvarapala figures; no excavated Chola sword is published that
I could find. The kit's "slightly curved south-Indian sword" should
become "a straight, broad, double-edged sword with a bronze or brass
grip and disc pommel, in a wooden scabbard." Iron blades, bronze or brass
fittings. [plausible] The flexible urumi is a Kerala tradition documented
only much later. [plausible] Spear and shield was the ordinary infantry
pairing; sword and shield for close work; bows for massed fire; elephants
and some cavalry for shock. [plausible] from the regiment names, which
separate archers, cavalry and elephant corps. Silambam as a system is not
attested for 1010. [plausible]

## 6. Daily texture

**Money.** The Thanjavur inscriptions reckon values in kasu and in kalanju,
a weight of gold, and the game's "kasu" is therefore the right word.
[documented] SII II (Balasubrahmanyam 1975, which quotes these
valuations; I did not open SII II itself). Rajaraja's gold coins of the
Lanka occupation are about 4.3 g and 20 mm. [documented] coins.lakdiva.org
specimen record. The usual design of Chola gold and copper of this reign is
a standing king on one side and a seated figure on the other with a short
Nagari legend; the older Chola type shows the tiger with twin fish and a
bow. [plausible] from standard catalogs I did not open (Mitchiner;
Biddulph). For the pickup sprite: a thick round coin, slightly irregular
edge, a standing figure, no lettering legible at game scale.

**Food.** Rice in every form; the temple offering lists specify rice, ghee,
curd, vegetables, plantains, areca and betel, sugar, pepper. [documented]
SII II offering inscriptions (as summarized in Balasubrahmanyam 1975).
Fish on the coast. [plausible] Toddy from palmyra. [plausible]

**Music and sound.** Drums (the inscriptions distinguish several), conch,
cymbals, flute, the yal or vina, and sung Tevaram hymns in the temple;
work songs on the shore. [documented] musicians and singers in SII II
no. 66; instruments [plausible].

**Religion in daily life.** The court was Shaiva; the king's own port had
a Buddhist monastery he endowed; Vaishnava and Jain shrines stood in the
same towns. [documented] Leiden plates; SII II. Processions with the
bronze image on a palanquin, lamps at dusk, offerings of flowers and
sandal. [plausible]

**Language and writing.** Tamil in Tamil script for the eulogy and the
grant; Sanskrit in Grantha for the opening verses. [documented] the
Thanjavur and Leiden records are bilingual this way. A wall inscription
is neat horizontal lines of rounded Tamil letters cut into dressed granite
around a plinth at eye level, no spaces between words. [documented] the
Thanjavur inscriptions' physical form. A sample opening: "Tirumagal pola
perunilacchelviyum," the first words of Rajaraja's eulogy. [documented]
Sastri 1955.

**Forms of address.** The king is "perumal" or "udaiyar"; a commander
"nayakar"; respectful suffix "-ar." [plausible] from inscriptional usage
summarized by Sastri and Subbarayalu.

## 7. Things to avoid

- **Later gopurams and Nayak color.** Soaring painted gopurams, Nayak
  pillared halls and the 1777 fort wall post-date the game. Keep the 1010
  fabric: vimana, two low gates, cloister, Nandi.
- **Mughal and Vijayanagara arms.** No talwar, chain mail, plumed steel
  helmets or lamellar. Bare torsos, cloth, hide shields, straight swords,
  spears.
- **Dhows and junks.** Lateen sails and iron-fastened hulls are Arab and
  later; sewn planks and square or canted sails are right.
- **Borobudur in Sumatra.** Different island, different kingdom. Brick
  candi and stilt houses.
- **"Navy" as an institution.** Say ships and fleet.
- **Modern nationalism.** "Tamil conquest of Southeast Asia" is a
  twentieth-century framing. The raid was a raid; the guilds that followed
  were pan-South-Indian; the Cholas left no colonies. Write Rajendra's
  boast as a boast.
- **Hindu versus Buddhist.** Rajaraja endowed a Buddhist monastery for a
  Buddhist foreign king; the raid had no religious motive. Do not stage
  level 3 as a temple-breaking war.
- **Faceless Srivijayans.** They had a king with a Sanskrit name, Old Malay
  inscriptions, a Chinese embassy three years after the raid, and fighters
  the Chinese called skilled. Give them faces, gongs, and a gateway worth
  seizing.
- **Ponniyin Selvan as history.** Fiction set fifty years earlier; its
  characters are not sources.

## 9. Questions I could not answer

1. What a Chola sailor or soldier wore on a ship in 1025; the kit's dress
   is extrapolated from sculpture of gods.
2. The size and crewing of the 1025 fleet, and whether ships were rowed.
3. Whether the Brihadisvara vimana was plastered or painted in 1010.
4. The plan and height of the Chudamani vihara.
5. Whether the Five Hundred of Ayyavole traded at Nagapattinam in
   1015–1025, or only Manigramam and Nanadesi.
6. The coin design of Rajaraja's gold kasu; weight and size verified only.
7. Any excavated Chola sword, spear or shield; all weapon claims rest on
   sculpture and poetry.
8. The regnal year and SII II number of the Thanjavur inscription that
   first carries the Kadaram passage.
9. Palembang's waterfront in 1025 beyond "wood, brick, rafts."
10. For the creative director: whether to treat the thirteen places as one
    1025 campaign (as here, following Kulke) or a composite of several.
