## 1. How to read this

Every factual sentence carries a tag. **[documented]** means a primary source
(inscription, contemporary text, excavation) or a peer-reviewed secondary
source that cites one, named inline. **[plausible]** means consistent with
the evidence but not directly attested, with the inference stated.
**[invented]** means history is silent and the game must choose; I say what
is least wrong. Section 8 says which sources were read in full and which
through surveys. Best general reads: Nilakanta Sastri, *The Cōḷas* (1955);
Kulke, Kesavapany and Sakhuja (eds.), *Nagapattinam to Suvarnadwipa*
(2009); Michell, *Architecture and Art of Southern India* (1995).

## 3. The three settings

### 3.1 Thanjavur, c. 1010

**Physical environment.** The temple enclosure is a walled rectangle about
240 m east–west by 120 m north–south, entered from the east through two
gateways in line, the Keralantakan tiruvasal and the Rajarajan tiruvasal,
both barrel-vaulted and well under half the vimana's height. [documented]
UNESCO nomination file (2004) for the dimensions; Balasubrahmanyam 1975,
Thanjavur chapter (wisdomlib.org), for the gateways. The sanctum is
30.18 m square at the base and rises in 13 storeys to an octagonal cupola
and a single granite capstone 7.77 m square. [documented] Balasubrahmanyam
1975. Published heights vary: Balasubrahmanyam 63.41 m, UNESCO 65 m, the
ASI figure 59.82 m repeated on Wikipedia. [plausible] as a range; the
game's "sixty-six metres" should read "over sixty metres." The copper
finial (stupi) is "said to be" 3.81 m; a recent cleaning measured 3.35 m.
[plausible] Balasubrahmanyam 1975. In 1010 it carried 2,926½ karanju of
gold plate over a copper pot. [documented] SII II no. 1, para. 18. Granite
throughout. [documented] Balasubrahmanyam 1975. A two-storied pillared
cloister lines the wall and a monolithic Nandi faces the sanctum; whether
the cloister's upper storey is Rajaraja's work is not settled, so the
level's "far path" along a gallery is [plausible] for 1010. My first
draft's "450 m" cloister is withdrawn. The outer fort wall and filled moat
are eighteenth-century and must not appear. [plausible] standard in the
guides; no primary date found.

In 1010 the stone was new, and the gopurams were lower than any
Vijayanagara or Nayak gopuram the artist has seen; Madurai and Srirangam
are 16th–17th century. [plausible] Whether the vimana was plastered or
painted in 1010 is unknown. [plausible] Do not add red lacquer to exterior
carvings as the stone texture prompt asks; keep "faint red" for interior
murals, which are Chola work, mostly Shaiva, with one royal scene usually
read as Rajaraja with his guru. [documented] Sriraman 2010, pp. 167–172
(via survey).

**People.** By Rajaraja's 29th year (c. 1014), SII II no. 66 had settled
400 temple women, "evidently dancing-girls" in Hultzsch's words, in
numbered houses on the streets around the temple, and about 110 men:
dance teachers, singers, drummers, pipers, lamp-lighters, washermen,
tailors, jewelers, potters, carpenters, parasol bearers, astrologers,
accountants; each paid in shares of land yielding 100 kalam of paddy.
[documented] SII II no. 66 (whatisindia.com). No. 65 of the same year
appoints 48 singers (pidarar) of the Tevaram hymns with two drummers.
[documented] SII II no. 65. Both postdate the 1010 scene by three or four
years; a 1010 courtyard is thinner, the dancers' streets still being
built. [plausible] Soldiers of named regiments made gifts and stood guard
at the gates; see section 4. [documented] SII II, introduction and no. 57
n. 2.

**Who is Rajendra in 1010.** A grown commander two years from co-regency
(1012). [documented] Sastri 1955, pp. 194–195. His birth year ("c. 971")
is speculation. [plausible] Fix "young prince" to "the king's son and
general."

**Sounds and objects.** Oil lamps everywhere: the inscriptions record
gifts of sheep and cows whose ghee fed perpetual lamps. [documented] SII
II no. 66 and the many lamp grants. Conch, drums, cymbals, Tevaram
singing. [documented] SII II no. 65. Parasols, fly-whisks, garlands, brass
vessels, sandal paste, camphor flame. [plausible] The gate prop's "oil lamp
in a niche" is unattested; the period lamp is a standing or hanging bronze
lamp. Put a small standing lamp at the pillar base. [plausible]

**The torana gate prop.** A free-standing stone torana with an upswept
curved lintel is a North Indian and Buddhist form (Sanchi); the Chola
gateway is the gopura or a plain lintelled doorway. [plausible] no Chola
torana of this shape found. The prop is [invented]; least wrong is two
granite pillars with a straight lintel and a cloth pennant, which also
reads as the "jeweled gate" of Srivijaya in level 3.

**What the game gets wrong here.** (a) "Sixty-six metres." (b) "Young
prince." (c) The backdrop's "many tiered gopuram towers": two, both low.
(d) Crimson banners on the walls: the attested flag is the kodi on a
flagstaff; streamers off walls are [invented]. (e) "Granite vimana rises
over the city" needs no change. (f) "Emperor Rajendra": Chola records call
the king perumal, udaiyar or ko; "emperor" is a modern convenience.
[documented] usage in SII II. Tolerable in English; "the king" is truer.

### 3.2 Nagapattinam, c. 1015–1025

**The port.** Nagapattinam was the Chola kingdom's main eastern harbor and
the entry point for traders and envoys from Sri Lanka, Burma, the Malay
peninsula, Sumatra and Java. [documented] Seshadri in Kulke 2009, ch. 7;
Fontein 1980. Song-period Chinese sherds along the Coromandel coast show
the China trade running through these ports. [documented] Karashima in
Kulke 2009, ch. 2. Flat sandy coast, Kaveri delta behind: no cliffs, no
hills, river mouths and backwaters. [plausible] from the geography.

**The vihara.** The Chudamani vihara was begun by the Srivijayan king
Chudamanivarman and completed by his son Mara Vijayottungavarman, with
Rajaraja's consent and his grant of Anaimangalam village in 1006.
[documented] Larger Leiden plates (Mahalingam 1948; Goh and Miksic 2016).
Buddhist, foreign-funded, in a Shaiva king's chief port: the game should
show it. More than 350 Buddhist bronzes of the 11th to 16th centuries have
been found at Nagapattinam. [documented] Fontein 1980. A tall brick tower
of the complex stood until Jesuits demolished it in 1867. [documented]
Mahalingam 1948. Its plan is unrecorded; a tall square plastered brick
tower with a tiered top is the least wrong rendering. [plausible]

**Traders and guilds.** The "Five Hundred Lords of Ayyavole" existed and
operated overseas: their Tamil inscription of 1088 at Lobu Tua near Barus,
Sumatra, records the "Five Hundred of the thousand directions" meeting
there. [documented] Lobu Tua inscription (Christie 1998). Ayyavole is
Aihole in Karnataka; the guild was pan-South-Indian, not Tamil by origin.
[documented] same. Their presence at Nagapattinam in 1015–1025 is not
attested; Manigramam, Nanadesi and the local nagaram merchants are safer
names. [plausible] from the 9th-century Takuapa inscription and
Champakalakshmi. Foreigners in port: Sinhalese, Malays, Burmese monks, Arab
and Persian merchants, Chinese crews. [plausible]

**Cargo.** The 1015 and 1033 embassies carried pearls, tusks and
frankincense to China. [documented] *Song Shi* 489. Cotton textiles went
to Southeast Asia. [documented] Devare in Kulke 2009, ch. 11 (title only).
Inbound: Chinese ceramics, camphor and benzoin from Barus, tin.
[plausible] War elephants loaded for export, as the level text says, are
unattested; the Cholas took elephants from Kadaram. [invented] Replace
with "pearls, cloth and rice."

**Ships.** Indian Ocean hulls of this period were sewn-plank: planks
stitched edge to edge with coir cord through drilled holes, no iron nails.
[documented] Staples and Blue 2019; Marco Polo and Ibn Battuta on the
nail-less ships of Malabar. Southeast Asian hulls used the related
stitched-plank and lashed-lug method, with several masts, canted
rectangular sails and sometimes outriggers, as on the Borobudur reliefs
(c. 800). [plausible] Manguin (not opened). The `tex_wood.png` "iron nail
heads" are wrong; ask for "zigzag seams of dark coir cord along the plank
edges, wooden dowels." The `l2_far.png` "lateen-style sails" belong to
Arab dhows; Indian and Malay sails were square or canted rectangles of
matting or cotton. [plausible] Sakhuja and Sakhuja (Kulke 2009, ch. 4)
argue the fleet was requisitioned merchant shipping; Subbarayalu (ch. 5)
finds no evidence of a standing Chola navy. [documented] as reported in
the volume; chapters not read. The ship names "kalamukha," "manthai" and
"sandhani" that circulate online rest on no Chola text. [plausible]

**A dock scene an artist can draw.** Low flat coast, late afternoon. Broad
beamy hulls drawn up on sand or anchored in the river mouth, planks
visibly stitched, one or two masts with matting sail furled, no iron
fittings. Catamarans moving cargo. Thatched sheds and plastered brick
warehouses; bales of cloth, rice baskets, jars, ceramic bowls. Tamil
porters in short dhotis and head cloths; a merchant in white dhoti with
gold earrings; a monk in ochre; a Malay trader in a waist cloth; an Arab
in a long robe; a Chinese sailor in jacket and trousers. A brick Buddhist
tower behind; palms. All [plausible] except the hulls and vihara,
[documented] above.

### 3.3 Srivijaya and Kadaram, 1025

**What the raid was.** Rajendra's Thanjavur inscription says he sent "many
ships in the midst of the rolling sea," captured Sangrama Vijayottungavarman
"the king of Kadaram" with his elephants and treasure, seized the "jeweled
wicket-gate" (vidyadhara-torana) at the war gate of Srivijaya, and took
twelve more places, among them Pannai, Malaiyur, Ilangasoka,
Talaittakkolam and Manakkavaram. [documented] Thanjavur inscription of
Rajendra; Tiruvalangadu plates, 14th regnal year (Kulke 2009, pp. 1–19;
Sastri 1955, pp. 211–220). Srivijaya is Palembang; Kadaram is Kedah;
Pannai is Panai in Sumatra; Malaiyur is Jambi; Ilangasoka is Langkasuka;
Manakkavaram is the Nicobars. [documented] Coedes 1968; several disputed.
A raid, not a conquest: no Chola administration followed. [documented]
Kulke 2009; Spencer 1983. The motive is unsettled: Sastri proposed
Srivijayan interference with Chola trade or Rajendra's wish to extend his
conquests; Spencer the "politics of plunder"; Sen a trade war over access
to Song China. [documented] Kulke 2009, pp. 1–19. Fleet size, route and
crossing time are recorded nowhere. [invented] "Many ships" is the only
honest phrase.

**The Srivijayan side.** Srivijaya was a Malay Buddhist thalassocracy
centered on Palembang on the Musi river, with Jambi, Kedah and the
peninsular ports in its network. [documented] Yijing (c. 690); Coedes 1968.
Chinese sources describe people living partly on rafts and houses over
the water, skilled fighters on land and sea who supplied their own gear,
and a port that forced passing ships to call and attacked those that did
not. [documented] Zhao Rugua, *Zhufan zhi* (1225), via Sen in Kulke 2009;
two centuries after the raid. Srivijaya's kings corresponded with Song
China, had funded a monastery in Rajaraja's own port, and sent an embassy
to China in 1028. [documented] *Song Shi* 489; Larger Leiden plates. The
kingdom survived; its center drifted to Jambi, and Virarajendra raided
Kedah again in 1068. [documented] Kulke 2009; Sastri 1955. The inscription
says nothing about the captured king's fate or Palembang's. [plausible]
silence, not evidence. The end screen's "Kadaram has fallen" is a Chola
boast; DECISIONS.md 4 adds a line of cost. Its "only great overseas naval
expedition in Indian history" is false: Sri Lanka (993, 1017) and Kedah
(1068) were also overseas. [documented] Sastri 1955. VISION.md's
replacement, "the largest overseas expedition any Indian ruler is known to
have sent," is [plausible]. "Trade with Song China flowed free" rests on
the 1033 and 1077 missions. [documented] *Song Shi* 489 for 1033; 1077
[plausible] via Sastri. "Tamil merchant guilds rose across Southeast Asia"
contradicts section 7: the Barus guild of 1088 was the pan-South-Indian
Ayyavole Five Hundred, writing in Tamil. [documented] Lobu Tua. Fix:
"South Indian merchant guilds."

**Landscape and architecture.** Kedah's port of this period is Sungai Mas
(5th–11th century, mostly Buddhist finds) and, from the 11th century,
Pengkalan Bujang (mainly Hindu finds) on the Merbok estuary: mangrove
coast, low river plain, Gunung Jerai (about 1,200 m) rising behind.
[documented] Bujang Valley survey (Jacq-Hergoualc'h 1992; Quaritch Wales
1940). Shrines are small brick and laterite candi on stepped plinths;
Candi Bukit Batu Pahat is the best known. [documented] same. Palembang's
monuments are brick and the early city was largely wood on stilts and on
the river. [documented] Zhao Rugua; Bukit Seguntang (Coedes 1968). The
`l3_far.png` "stupa sanctuaries in the manner of Borobudur" is wrong by
place and style: Borobudur is central Java, a different kingdom.
[documented] Fix: "low square brick and laterite shrines on stepped
plinths among stilt houses on a mangrove river"; no tall towers. The level's "stupa terraces" can stay as a ruined brick plinth. The
"citadel of the maharaja" is [invented]; a timber palisade with a carved
gateway (the inscription's own torana) is least wrong and justifies the
gate prop. The "monsoon jungle," "steep jungle ridges" and "ravine" with a
rope bridge are [invented] against a mangrove plain; fix: one forested
mountain in the far plane, mist over flat water in the mid plane, a river
crossing on stilt walkways. Rain is fair. [plausible] The level 3 enemy
must be Srivijayan, not the Chola guard sprite (DECISIONS.md 3).

**People and dress.** Malay men in a wrapped waist cloth to the knee, bare
chest, a head cloth; chiefs with gold. [plausible] The kris is not securely
attested before the 13th century; prefer a straight short sword or spear.
[plausible] Buddhist monks in robes; Sanskrit and Old Malay on stone.
[documented] Old Malay inscriptions of the 680s (Kedukan Bukit, Talang
Tuwo). Sounds: river water, bronze gongs, cicadas, rain. [plausible]

## 4. People and roles

**Rajaraja I** (r. 985–1014), born Arulmolivarman, titles Mummudi Cholan,
Keralantakan, Sivapadasekharan; eulogy "Tirumagal pola." [documented]
Sastri 1955. **Rajendra I** (co-regent 1012, r. 1014–1044), titles
Gangaikonda Cholan, Kadaram-kondan ("who took Kadaram"), Mudigonda Cholan;
eulogy "Tirumanni valara." [documented] Sastri 1955. The level text's
"Ganges-conqueror" renders Gangaikonda fairly; "lord of the eastern sea"
is a paraphrase, not a title. [plausible]

**Army organization.** The Thanjavur inscriptions name regiments by royal
title: Keralantaka-vasal-tirumeykappar and Anukka-vasal-tirumeykappar
(bodyguards of the Keralantaka gate and the inner gate), Pandita-Chola-
terinda-villigal (archers), Nigarili-Chola-terinda-udanilai-kudiraicchevagar
(cavalry), Mummadi-Chola-terinda-anaippagar (elephant corps), and
thirteen Valangai-Velaikkarar units; 31 regiments in all appear as donors.
[documented] SII II, Hultzsch's introduction and no. 57 n. 2, which glosses
tirumeykappu as "the sacred body-guard" in three divisions, two at the
Keralantaka and Anukka gates. "Archers," "cavalry" and "elephant corps"
are Sastri's readings of the Tamil. [plausible] A gift to the writer: the
guards at level 1's temple gate are the "tirumeykappar of the Keralantaka
gate." Commanders held titles such as senapati, nayakan and dandanayakan.
[plausible] Subbarayalu (not opened). "Velaikkarar" were the king's sworn
troops; the "suicide corps" reading is not secure. [documented] Sastri
1955; Stein. A common soldier was a "sevagan." [plausible]

**The hero.** "Warrior of the Chola navy" presumes a navy; the evidence is
for ships and soldiers put on them. [documented] Subbarayalu in Kulke
2009, ch. 5. Better: "a soldier of the fleet." "Ilamaran" ("young" plus
the Pandya royal name Maran) is unattested for a Chola soldier but a fair
invention; I do not object. [plausible] His kit dress (bare chest, tucked
dhoti, cross-belt, topknot, earrings, armlets, bangles) matches Chola
bronze conventions. [plausible] from sculpture of gods and donors, not
sailors. Footwear: sculpture shows bare feet; sandals are unattested
for a common soldier. [plausible] For the redraw: bare feet in all five
poses. "Crimson silk sash": silk was imported and costly; cotton.
[plausible] Keep the color, drop "silk."

**The tiger.** The Chola emblem is the tiger: the seal on Chola
copper-plate grants shows a seated tiger with two fish and a bow (the
Pandya and Chera emblems the Cholas claimed). [documented] seal of the
10th-century Chola plates, Government Museum, Chennai. The tiger flag
(puli-kkodi) is the standard image of Chola kingship in Tamil literature;
no textile survives. [plausible] "The Sea Belongs to the Tiger" and the
"tiger banner" are sound as a Chola boast; draw a tiger on a crimson field
on a pole, not a heraldic shield.

**The guard.** Tested item by item.
- "Crimson lamellar armor, rows of lacquered plates over a dark tunic":
  [invented], and the wrong invention. No Chola sculpture, inscription or
  foreign account shows lamellar in South India; figures in Chola reliefs
  fight bare-chested. Lacquered lamellar is Tibetan, Chinese and Central
  Asian. Armor itself existed for elites: Sangam-era literature has
  generals in "coats of mail" and arsenals holding "breast-plates"
  (Kanakasabai 1956 ed., p. 131, citing *Silappadikaram* XV), eight
  centuries before the game and never for a gate guard. [plausible] Least
  wrong: bare chest, crossed belt, crimson cloth around the torso, or a
  quilted cotton jacket if the silhouette needs armor ([plausible];
  attested later in South India).
- "Conical bronze helmet with a red plume": [invented]. No helmet is
  attested. Dvarapalas wear crowns; soldiers in relief wear a cloth head
  band, turban or bare topknot. Least wrong: a crimson head cloth, or a
  plain iron skullcap if readability needs a hard head.
- "Round dark-wood shield slung on the back": [plausible]. Sangam-era
  literature gives foot soldiers "large bucklers made of ox-hide" and a
  lance or battle-axe, cavalry shorter bucklers (Kanakasabai 1956 ed.,
  pp. 130–131, citing *Silappadikaram* XV); hero stones show round and
  oblong shields. Make it hide over wicker, with a boss.
- "Long spear with a leaf-shaped iron head": [plausible]. The vel is the
  standard Tamil weapon from Sangam poetry onward; the leaf blade is the
  form in Murugan iconography.
- "Chola imperial guard": [documented] the tirumeykappar regiments above.
  "Imperial" is modern; "royal bodyguard" or "gate guard."

## 5. Weapons and combat

Tamil sources from the Sangam anthologies on give a stable vocabulary: vel
(spear), val (sword), vil and ambu (bow and arrow), kedayam or kidugu
(shield), and the war drum. [documented] Purananuru and Patirruppattu as
surveyed in Sastri 1955 and Kanakasabai (1904; 1956 ed.); poems not read
this session. Kanakasabai is a colonial-era synthesis that reads Sangam
poetry as reportage: a pointer to the poems, not a witness, and 800 years
before the game.

Two sword forms are period, side by side. One is the broad, straight,
double-edged blade with a short grip and knob or disc pommel seen in Chola
bronzes of Durga, Bhairava and Kali. [plausible] from sculpture; no
excavated Chola sword is published that I could find. The other is a
forward-curved blade, edge on the convex side, widening toward the tip,
with a square cushion pommel: on a 7th-century Pallava gana at
Mamallapuram and, in Elgood's words, it "continues in use for centuries,"
with hilts of the type from at least the Chola period; its late descendant
is the sosun pattah. [documented] Elgood, *Hindu Arms and Ritual* (2015),
p. 83, as quoted by the fact-checker. Sangam swords were "broad in the
blade and about a yard long." [plausible] Kanakasabai 1956 ed., p. 131.
Only the back-curved talwar is out of period; it displaces the South
Indian forms after the 16th century. [documented] Elgood 2015. The kit's
"slightly curved south-Indian sword" is defensible if the curve is
forward; the redraw prompt should say "a straight broad double-edged
sword, or a forward-curved blade widening toward the tip; brass grip,
disc or cushion pommel, wooden scabbard; never a talwar." Spear and shield
was the ordinary infantry pairing. [plausible] from the regiment names.

## 6. Daily texture

**Money.** The Thanjavur inscriptions reckon values in kasu and in kalanju
(karanju), a gold weight, and paddy in kalam; the game's "kasu" is the
right word. [documented] SII II nos. 1, 65, 66. Rajaraja's gold coins of
the Lanka occupation are about 4.3 g and 20 mm, of pale low-fineness gold.
[documented] coins.lakdiva.org. Design: a standing king with a lamp stand
on one side, a seated figure holding a conch on the other, Devanagari
legend "Sri Raja Raja" beside the seated figure. [documented] Codrington
104; Mitchiner, *Southern India* pt. 2, no. 318, via a Spink sale record.
Gold was high value and copper kasu existed; a dozen gold kasu per level
is a game convention. [plausible] Pickup sprite: thick round coin, pale
gold, a standing figure, no legible lettering.

**Food and sound.** Temple offering lists specify rice, ghee, curd,
vegetables, plantains, areca and betel, sugar, pepper. [documented] SII II
offering inscriptions (via Balasubrahmanyam 1975). Drums and Tevaram
singers [documented] SII II nos. 65, 66; conch, flute, yal, work songs on
the shore [plausible].

**Religion in daily life.** A Shaiva court whose king endowed a Buddhist
monastery in his own port. [documented] Leiden plates; SII II.
Processions, lamps at dusk, flowers and sandal. [plausible]

**Language and writing.** Tamil in Tamil script for the eulogy and grant;
Sanskrit in Grantha for the opening verses. [documented] the Thanjavur and
Leiden records. A wall inscription is neat horizontal lines of rounded
Tamil letters cut into dressed granite around a plinth at eye level, no
spaces between words. [documented] SII II. A sample opening: "Tirumagal
pola perunilacchelviyum," the first words of Rajaraja's eulogy.
[documented] Sastri 1955. The king is "perumal" or "udaiyar"; a commander
"nayakar"; respectful suffix "-ar." [plausible]

## 7. Things to avoid

- **Later gopurams and Nayak color.** Soaring painted gopurams, Nayak
  halls and the fort wall post-date the game. Keep the 1010 fabric.
- **Mughal and Vijayanagara arms.** No talwar, chain mail, plumed steel
  helmets or lamellar. Bare torsos, cloth, hide shields, straight or
  forward-curved swords, spears.
- **Dhows, junks, Borobudur, "navy."** See 3.2 and 3.3.
- **Modern nationalism.** "Tamil conquest of Southeast Asia" is a
  twentieth-century framing. The raid was a raid; the guilds were
  pan-South-Indian; the Cholas left no colonies. Write the boast as a boast.
- **Hindu versus Buddhist.** The raid had no religious motive (3.2, 3.3).
- **Faceless Srivijayans.** See 3.3. Give them faces, gongs, and a gateway
  worth seizing.
- **Ponniyin Selvan and Kanakasabai as history.** The novel is fiction;
  Kanakasabai (1904) is a colonial-era reading of Sangam poetry as a golden
  age. Neither is a witness.
