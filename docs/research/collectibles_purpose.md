# How 2D platformers and action games give collectibles a purpose (for the kasu coins in the Chola voyage game)

Research note: about 20 tool calls. Several fan-wiki pages could not be fetched because permission requests timed out: mariowiki.com/Animal_Token and celestegame.fandom.com/wiki/Collectibles. Some important claims below (Celeste's strawberry text, DKC2 banana coins, DKC3 bear coins, Hollow Knight geo, Ori spirit light, Shovel Knight gems) are well known but **could not be confirmed against a source in this pass**. ## Q1. What do the collectibles in DKC 1-3, The Jungle Book (SNES), The Lost Crown and AC Chronicles: India actually do?

### Takeaway
In DKC every pickup turns into **extra lives**, and the hidden layer (bonus barrels and rooms, then Kremkoins and DK coins) turns into **access to secret content and a completion percentage**. Jungle Book gems are a **required exit quota**. In The Lost Crown, currencies are **spent on concrete upgrades and items** at vendors. In AC Chronicles: India, collectibles are side tasks and **scoring is a per-mission stealth grade**. That last one is exactly the end-of-level tally the Chola design rule forbids, and critics did not find it meaningful.

### Cited Findings
**Donkey Kong Country (1994)**
- Bananas: 100 bananas give an extra life. All four K-O-N-G letters in a level give an extra life. Balloons each give an extra life. Wikipedia says collecting three animal tokens of the same kind "grants an extra life" — [Wikipedia: Donkey Kong Country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donkey_Kong_Country). (Note: the widely known mechanic is that three matching tokens warp you to an animal bonus stage where you collect more tokens for lives. Wikipedia compresses this, and I could not fetch the Mario Wiki animal-token page to confirm.)
- Some items sit on the main path and others in hidden bonus stages "where you earn them by solving puzzles". Finding bonus stages raises the completion percentage, and the maximum of 101% unlocks a different ending — [Wikipedia: DKC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donkey_Kong_Country)
- Design intent: Nintendo asked Rare to lower the difficulty for a broad audience, reasoning that **the secrets would give hardcore players their challenge**. Critics split: EGM and GamePro found bonus stages hard to find, and GamePro praised them for replay value — [Wikipedia: DKC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donkey_Kong_Country)
- KONG letters appear in every non-boss level, sometimes hidden or inside bonus areas. Only the GBA remake requires all letters, for 101% — [Super Mario Wiki: KONG Letters](https://www.mariowiki.com/KONG_Letters)
- When a level's bonus barrels are cleared, the map marks it: DKC1 and DKC2 add an exclamation mark to the level name, and DKC3 flies the level flag fully. Bonus areas give rare collectibles (Kremkoins, DK coins, bonus coins) that "unlock secret worlds and count toward completion percentage" — [Mario Fandom: Bonus Barrel](https://mario.fandom.com/wiki/Bonus_Barrel)

**Donkey Kong Country 2 (1995)**
- 100 bananas give a life. **Kremkoins** come from beating bonus challenges, and Klubba's Kiosk demands a number of them before you can enter the **Lost World**, where DK coins are collected — [Wikipedia: DKC2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donkey_Kong_Country_2:_Diddy%27s_Kong_Quest)
- The Kong family offer services: Cranky gives advice and secrets, Wrinkly gives guidance and saves, Swanky runs a quiz show for extra lives, and Funky flies you between worlds. 102% requires all levels and bosses, all bonus challenges, all Lost World DK coins, and visiting the four Kongs — [Wikipedia: DKC2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donkey_Kong_Country_2:_Diddy%27s_Kong_Quest)
- The DKC2 manual says: "Collect the letters to spell KONG and earn an extra life." — [Super Mario Wiki: KONG Letters](https://www.mariowiki.com/KONG_Letters)

**The Jungle Book (SNES/Genesis, 1994)**
- Each level holds 15 gems. The quota needed to progress is 8 on easy, 10 on medium and 12 on hard. You then find a specific character to exit, or fight a boss every other level. Each level has a six-minute limit. Power-ups include invincibility masks, coconuts, double-banana and boomerang-banana projectiles. Points come from gems and items. (The Wikipedia article is based mainly on the Genesis version, and says the SNES version's gameplay is the same though some level design differs.) — [Wikipedia: The Jungle Book (video game)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Jungle_Book_(video_game))

**Prince of Persia: The Lost Crown (2024)**
- The game has three currencies. Xerxes coins are rarer than Time Crystals and are found mainly by thorough exploration. The Mage at The Haven also sells one Xerxes for 200 Time Crystals — [Gfinity](https://www.gfinityesports.com/guides/prince-of-persia-the-lost-crown-xerxes/)
- Xerxes coins are spent at **the Scrapper** in the Depths, who sells powerful amulets and items such as chest keys. Most coins lie along the main path and some come from side quests. Time Crystals dropped by enemies are a separate currency spent at shopkeepers — [Prima Games](https://primagames.com/gaming/where-to-spend-the-xerxes-coins-in-prince-of-persia-the-lost-crown)

**Assassin's Creed Chronicles: India (2016)**
- "you're scored for each mission depending on your level of stealth". Unlocks include Helix powers and challenge rooms. Critique: "all of the most interesting efforts to fight the good fight are reduced to finding collectibles" — [GameSpot review](https://www.gamespot.com/reviews/assassins-creed-chronicles-india-review/1900-6416328/)

### Inferences
- DKC's satisfaction has two layers. The surface trail of bananas guides the eye and pays out often in small amounts (lives). The hidden layer (bonus rooms, then coins, then a secret world and an ending) pays for curiosity with **places**, not points. The second layer works with no score counter at all, because the map shows it (the "!" marks and the flag).
- Jungle Book's quota is the clearest case of coins that "have a point". They are the key that opens the exit. It also shows the risk: a quota turns exploration into a chore, especially with a timer.
- The Lost Crown and AC India sit at opposite ends. Lost Crown currency gives the player power. AC India's score and grade is the tally model the Chola game has ruled out, and its collectibles were criticised as a substitute for story.

### Gaps
- I found no first-hand Rare interview on why bonus rooms and tokens exist. The Nintendo "secrets for hardcore players" point comes through Wikipedia.
- Not confirmed against a source: DKC2 **banana coins** paid to the Kong family for services, DKC3 **bear coins** and **banana birds** (uncited, from general knowledge). Jungle Book SNES-specific items such as 1-up hearts, the bonus stage and checkpoint behaviour. The full Lost Crown Xerxes count, and Time Crystal sinks (amulet and weapon upgrades at Kaheh are uncited).
- AC Chronicles: India's specific collectible types (for example scrolls or chests) are not described in the sources I read.

## Q2. What design functions do collectibles serve, and which ones work without a visible score counter?

### Takeaway
Collectibles do six jobs: guiding the eye, paying for exploration, creating risk-reward detours, acting as lives or currency, unlocking things or opening gates, and carrying lore or completion. Only the score or tally job *needs* a counter. Every other job can be shown through the world, the map, a vendor, or a gate.

### Cited Findings
- Game Wisdom groups collectibles into extrinsic rewards (health, attack, resources), lore collectibles (they "include a way for players to track and review them"), progression-linked ones (Mario 64 coins leading to stars), and optional filler. Its rules: every single collectible should give *something*, even "+.5% DPS"; rewards should scale with progress, because flat currency like "$500 a collectible" loses value; reward individual items, not only full sets; avoid items players cannot find. In small linear levels players find items by looking. Mini-map markers "minimize the impact of the hunt". Proximity cues such as Crackdown orbs' sound and glow are a good model — [Game Wisdom: The Craft of Collectible Design](https://game-wisdom.com/critical/collectible-design-videogames)
- DKC uses map markers rather than numbers to show that a level's secrets are cleared ("!" after the level name, or a fully raised flag) — [Mario Fandom: Bonus Barrel](https://mario.fandom.com/wiki/Bonus_Barrel)

### Inferences (applying this to the kasu)
- **Guiding the eye** (the current use) works without a counter, but on its own it gives the coins no point. Players read them as "the developer's breadcrumbs".
- **Spendable currency** works without an on-screen counter if the balance is shown only at the spending point, the way Lost Crown keeps currency at vendors. Historical fit: kasu could pay a port official's toll, buy provisions or a pilot at a port, or be given as a temple donation. Chola inscriptions record gold gifts to temples, but that needs its own sourcing.
- **Exit gate or quota** (Jungle Book) works without a counter if the gate shows the amount itself, for example a toll-keeper who waves you through once paid. It is a risky choice for a game with an educational aim: "pay to proceed" feels like a chore.
- **Risk-reward detours** (DKC bonus rooms, Lost Crown exploration coins) need no counter. The reward is a hidden space plus something placed there.
- **Unlocks and access** (DKC2 Kremkoins opening the Lost World) need no counter. Kasu could open a hidden scene: a merchant guild hall, a shrine, a vignette about Srivijaya trade.
- **Lore** needs no counter, only a place to review it later (Game Wisdom's "track and review").
- **Completion** can be shown on the voyage map as marks, like DKC's "!", rather than as numbers.
- **Lives** are probably wrong for an educational game that has no score. They bring back the arcade tally model.

### Gaps
- I found no Game Maker's Toolkit transcript on collectibles in this pass.

## Q3. Collectibles that carry story or meaning in historical or narrative games

### Takeaway
The best-regarded "meaningful" collectibles are the ones whose content adds to the world: audio logs, photographs with vignettes, codex entries. Generic currency and minimap dots are the standard "pointless" examples.

### Cited Findings
- BioShock's audio logs expand the story and make Rapture feel lived in, though they can break tension. Mad Max's photographs, with handwritten vignettes about what happened to people, add to the narrative, while its scrap piles are only currency: "One of these adds some sort of value to the game's narrative, the other adds dots to the game's minimap." — [Game Developer: Collectibles: Subtraction by Addition](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/collectibles-subtraction-by-addition)
- The same article criticises Assassin's Creed feathers and flags as having little motivation, a "Sisyphean Pac-Man" of minimap dots — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/collectibles-subtraction-by-addition)
- Celeste calls its strawberries explicitly "optional" — [Jump Dash Roll](https://www.jumpdashroll.com/article/celeste-brutal-backlog). Celeste has 175 red berries, 26 golden berries and 1 moon berry. Thorson said the Farewell chapter would have "no alternate sides or strawberries" — [Celeste Wiki](https://celestegame.fandom.com/wiki/Celeste)

### Inferences
- In a historical game a coin is an artifact in its own right. The Chola kasu could carry the meaning: a coin from a specific reign or mint, or the coin a temple donor gave. Collecting a "special" kasu could unlock a short codex card. This follows the Mad Max photo model rather than the scrap model.

### Gaps (uncited, from general knowledge, verify before quoting)
- **Celeste:** an in-game message, commonly reported as saying strawberries "will impress your friends, but that's about it", frames them as pure bragging rights, a way to say "skill challenges, not progress gates". The ending is said to change slightly with the strawberry count. I could not reach a source with the exact wording or a Thorson interview.
- **Hollow Knight geo:** a currency for maps, charms and upgrades, dropped by your Shade when you die and recovered by defeating it. This is a risk-reward loop without a score. (Search found hollowknight.wiki/w/Shade but I did not fetch it.)
- **Ori's spirit light:** experience spent on the skill tree, and in Will of the Wisps a shop currency.
- **Shovel Knight gems:** a currency for upgrades. On death you drop some as floating money bags you can recover, and you can break checkpoints for extra gems, which is a risk-for-reward choice.
- **AC Codex/database entries:** contextual history cards unlocked as you encounter places and people. This is the most directly relevant educational precedent, but I did not get a source for it in this pass.
- I found no Ubisoft Montpellier interview on The Lost Crown's collectibles.

## Q4. What critics and designers say about pointless versus meaningful collectibles

### Takeaway
Critics call collectibles pointless when they are padding, minimap dots, flat currency, or rewards for a full set only, and when they interrupt pacing or replace the story. They call them meaningful when each one adds to the world or gives a tangible benefit that scales, and when finding it is a fair act of noticing.

### Cited Findings
- Tyler Corbine argues collectibles usually hurt narrative and pacing. In Journey, noticing a collectible made him wonder "if I would come across some sort of collectible" instead of being immersed. Modern Warfare intel interrupts firefights. Nintendo stars can gate difficulty, but hunting "every damn tree" breaks the intended pacing. Publishers use collectibles as a "phoned-in" way to pad playtime — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/collectibles-subtraction-by-addition)
- Good practice: make items stand out yet stay optional, reward every item, scale the rewards, keep finding them free of frustration. Bad practice: currency whose value collapses, rewards for the full set only, mandatory collecting outside main progression, items hidden beyond any fair cue — [Game Wisdom](https://game-wisdom.com/critical/collectible-design-videogames)
- AC Chronicles: India was criticised for reducing its most interesting story actions to collectible hunting — [GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/reviews/assassins-creed-chronicles-india-review/1900-6416328/)
- GamePro praised DKC bonus stages for replay value, while both it and EGM found them hard to find — [Wikipedia: DKC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donkey_Kong_Country)

### Inferences (options for the kasu with no counter and an educational aim, roughly best fit first)
1. **Kasu as spendable currency at ports** (the Lost Crown model, but diegetic). The balance shows only when you trade: buy a pilot's advice, a monsoon chart, provisions, or a temple offering. Every coin then has an obvious point.
2. **Detour coins that open a hidden vignette** (DKC bonus room plus Kremkoin/Lost World). A cluster of kasu off the main route marks a secret. A cleared secret marks the voyage map, like DKC's "!".
3. **Named or special kasu as artifacts** that unlock codex cards (the Mad Max photos and AC database model). Ordinary kasu stay as the trail, and a few rare ones teach history.
4. **Avoid:** lives, a score or grade (the AC India model), and a hard per-level quota (the Jungle Book model), which conflict with the design rule or make exploration a chore.
