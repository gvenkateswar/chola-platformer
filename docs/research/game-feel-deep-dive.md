# Give the rope a body, the sword a bite

The three complaints come from specific, fixable choices in the code. None of them is a matter of taste. **The rope looks like a stick because it is drawn as almost one straight line.** It is simulated as a single rigid pendulum that can bow by at most about 5 px. A hard clamp stops it dead at 50 degrees, and the hero's whole figure is welded to that line, so rope, arms, body and the tail below his hands all pivot as one rod. **The sword looks like a cricket-bat bunt because only the shoulder moves.** The blade is fixed to a straight forearm and swings about 110 degrees with an ease that is slowest at the start and end. There is no wind-up, wrist, hips, step, smear or hit-pause. On top of that, **the hero cannot reach the enemy from where the enemy stops**: his cut lands only within 69 px, center to center, while the fighter stops at 90 px and thrusts out to about 96 px. A player who swings when the fighter halts will miss and get hit. **The coins have no point because they look like an arcade reward and give nothing back.** Picking one up plays the same dark ink smudge used for a lane change, and the trail they are meant to form is often just a flat row on open floor. The reference games solve each problem in a known way. Developers who built swings that feel good kept a simple pendulum for play and made the bend visual. Good melee games freeze both fighters for a few frames on contact and give each outcome (hit, blocked, parried) its own reaction. Collectibles work without a counter when they lead to a place, open a secret, or carry meaning. The prioritized list at the end puts the sword range and hit-pause fixes first because they are cheap and address "not fun" directly, then the rope's visual layer and soft swing limit, then coin feedback and placement. A few items need the owner to rule on the project's standing rules first, and they are marked.

A note on evidence. Facts about the game come from a code audit of `index.html` on branch `build/levels-2-3` (3,083 lines, HEAD `0a97a56`), run headless at a stepped 60 Hz, and line numbers refer to that file. Facts about other games come from the cited web sources. Anything labeled **inference** is the research team's design judgment and has no source. Several gaps are real. No reliable source described the vine mechanics of The Jungle Book on SNES, or the pole and rope swings in Sands of Time or The Lost Crown. No source gave frame data for The Lost Crown's parry or hit-pause.

## What the reference games actually do well

### Swings: predictable rhythm first, realism never

The best-documented classic is Donkey Kong Country on SNES. Its swinging ropes **"constantly sway back and forth without a loss of momentum,"** and grabbing one anywhere **drops the Kong to its lower end**. He cannot climb a swinging rope. Static vertical ropes are a separate type used for climbing ([Super Mario Wiki: Rope](https://mariowiki.com/Rope)). The only skill is when to grab and when to let go, and the player reads the timing from the rope's steady rhythm. The later DKC games add pumping: tilting the stick swings the character, and **releasing at the top of the arc carries him farther** ([Super Mario Wiki: Swinging vine](https://www.mariowiki.com/Swinging_vine)). The Jungle Book leaned on vine swinging so heavily that GamePro called it "pretty repetitious, centering around his ability to swing on vines." Reviewers still praised its animation, controls and "huge levels" ([Wikipedia: The Jungle Book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Jungle_Book_(video_game))). That is a warning that one traversal verb, however pretty, needs variation from level to level. The Lost Crown's director says the team carried over Rayman's lessons on "musicality and rhythm," and describes "the action on-screen" as music ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/2024/01/02/prince-of-persia-the-lost-crown-time-is-an-ocean-in-a-storm)). In Sands of Time, the art director describes poles bolted to pillars as "a great way to add swinging animations... So we made constraints into fun" ([Ubisoft News](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/3oAqiLg5k7GazIw5OWYTO2)).

Developers who documented their own swing tuning agree that **feel beats physical accuracy**. The Ropeman developer tried a single physics hinge, which "feels slow and moves weirdly." He then tried a jointed chain, which looked more natural but "pulled back on itself." He shipped a single hinge plus tricks: **velocity carried across the grab, and a tangential push right after connecting** ([kvfrans: Ropeman](https://kvfrans.com/omakase-1-ropeman/)). The Krilling team found that plain left/right steering let players hang off to one side. Their fix was to **scale the pendulum's own acceleration when the player's input matches its direction**, so alternating left and right builds the swing ([Krilling dev blog 11](https://comic.gamedev.net/blogs/entry/2291868-krilling-dev-blog-11)). Chola already pumps this way, so the pump logic is sound. The problems are in the limit, the weight and the drawing.

### Swords: a readable duel, then a freeze on contact

The 1989 Prince of Persia is the right model for "show me what my sword does." Its sword sprites were rotoscoped from **six seconds of the Flynn and Rathbone duel** in *The Adventures of Robin Hood*, filmed in exact profile. The result: "the blades clash high and low in a kind of balletic rhythm" ([Animation Obsessive](https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/how-the-little-guy-moved)). Its moves were advance, retreat, slash, parry, and parry-then-slash, and **enemy swordsmen carried a health indicator like the Prince's** ([Wikipedia: Prince of Persia (1989)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_of_Persia_(1989_video_game))). Mechner wanted the danger to be "visceral" without being graphic. Losing a fight "wasn't particularly graphic," and death stayed close through a "mistimed parry" ([Ubisoft retrospective](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/6yLrRf7b0U1MBYxmlxE225/prince-of-persia-35th-anniversary-a-look-back-at-the-original-game)). That matches Chola's no-gore rule. The Lost Crown builds its combat on a **color-coded telegraph**. Yellow eyes mean the attack can be parried for a "massive, cinematic counter-attack," red eyes mean it must be dodged, and **parrying a basic attack stuns the enemy briefly** ([Ubisoft Montpellier](https://www.ubisoft.com/en-us/studio/montpellier/news/NvEbHCYiO8TjMNmfchQ6X/prince-of-persia-the-lost-crown-will-put-your-combat-platforming-and-puzzlesolving-skills-to-the-test)). Reviewers liked the defensive parry-and-dodge tests more than free-form juggling. They noted that the basic **3-hit string ends in a punishable recovery** and that careless swinging gets punished fast ([GamingTrend](https://gamingtrend.com/reviews/prince-of-persia-the-lost-crown-review-heir-to-elegance/)). AC Chronicles: India is the negative example. Its combat was called "a bit stilted" and "least interesting" ([God is a Geek](https://godisageek.com/reviews/assassins-creed-chronicles-india-review/)), and slow weapon-draw response was a specific complaint ([GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/reviews/assassins-creed-chronicles-india-review/1900-6416328/)).

The single strongest technique in the research is **hitstop**: a brief freeze of both fighters at the moment of contact. Smash Ultimate computes it as `floor(damage × 0.65 + 6)` frames at 60 fps, capped at 30, so **even a light hit freezes about 6 to 8 frames (100 to 130 ms)** ([SmashWiki: Hitlag](https://www.ssbwiki.com/Hitlag)). Sakurai explains the details. Attacker and victim freeze for the same time. The victim shakes **side to side on the ground and up and down in the air, with the shake shrinking over the freeze**. Hurtboxes stay put so the shake never causes a miss, and the victim blends into its hurt pose over 4 frames during the freeze ([Source Gaming](https://sourcegaming.info/2015/11/11/thoughts-on-hitstop-sakurais-famitsu-column-vol-490-1/)). A 2024 user study tested 0 to 0.7 s of hitstop. Sword-scene participants rated about **0.37 s pleasant on average and about 0.55 s or more unpleasant**, and they **preferred hitstop that varied over a fixed value** ([Tomizawa & Ishikawa, GRAPP 2024](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2024/124614/124614.pdf)). For the swing itself, pixel artist Raymond Schlitter advises the following. Keep anticipation short, because too much feels laggy. "A single smear frame is often enough." Hold the follow-through slightly longer than other frames, freeze briefly on the impact frame of strong hits, and **slightly lengthen the weapon at full extension**. His sample cut covers 90 degrees from overhead to straight ahead, and wide arcs read as strong ([Slynyrd Pixelblog 9](https://slynyrd.com/blog/2018/9/8/pixelblog-9-melee-attacks)). Hollow Knight pushes the player back slightly when the nail strikes something, which makes hits feel heavier ([WPI DGMI wiki: Recoil](https://dgmiwiki.wpi.edu/mechanic/recoil)). Sekiro frames every enemy move as having "a specific counter," and "a missed deflect seamlessly turns into a guard" ([SuperJump](https://www.superjumpmagazine.com/the-art-and-science-of-sekiros-combat/)).

### Collectibles: guidance, secrets, meaning, and only sometimes numbers

DKC's pickups turn into extra lives (100 bananas, the K-O-N-G letters, balloons). Its hidden layer of bonus rooms raises a completion percentage that **unlocks a different ending at 101%**. Nintendo asked Rare to lower the difficulty, on the reasoning that **the secrets would give hardcore players their challenge** ([Wikipedia: DKC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donkey_Kong_Country)). Crucially, DKC shows cleared secrets **on the map, not as a number**: an exclamation mark after the level name in DKC1 and DKC2, a fully raised flag in DKC3 ([Mario Fandom: Bonus Barrel](https://mario.fandom.com/wiki/Bonus_Barrel)). DKC2's Kremkoins open the Lost World ([Wikipedia: DKC2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donkey_Kong_Country_2:_Diddy%27s_Kong_Quest)). The Jungle Book's 15 gems per level are an **exit quota of 8, 10 or 12 by difficulty**, under a six-minute limit ([Wikipedia: The Jungle Book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Jungle_Book_(video_game))). The Lost Crown's Xerxes coins are found by exploring and **spent at a vendor for amulets and keys** ([Prima Games](https://primagames.com/gaming/where-to-spend-the-xerxes-coins-in-prince-of-persia-the-lost-crown)). AC Chronicles: India grades each mission on stealth, which is exactly the end-of-level tally Chola forbids, and a critic complained that its most interesting ideas were "reduced to finding collectibles" ([GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/reviews/assassins-creed-chronicles-india-review/1900-6416328/)). Two design essays agree on the rest. **Every single collectible should give something**, players should never hunt for items they cannot find, and proximity cues such as sound and glow help ([Game Wisdom](https://game-wisdom.com/critical/collectible-design-videogames)). A collectible earns its place when it adds to the world, the way Mad Max's photographs carry short stories about people. Its scrap piles are the counterexample: "One of these adds some sort of value to the game's narrative, the other adds dots to the game's minimap" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/collectibles-subtraction-by-addition)).

### Pacing: one gimmick per level, escalating

What players remember from DKC are the stages built around a single idea that keeps escalating: STOP barrels that put charging golems to sleep, runaway mine carts, a snowfall that grows into a blizzard over the course of the level ([Episodic Content Mag](https://episodiccontentmag.com/2016/02/19/dkcountry1_chapter2)). Rare's Chris Sutherland cites Mario's habit of "introducing new features at a steady rate," and notes that with Mario "you can often play at a faster pace as long as you are ready to react" (same source). A 2025 retrospective says the gimmick stages hold up and that **bonus rooms placed mid-level "completely bust the game's flow"** ([Indie Gamer Chick](https://indiegamerchick.com/2025/07/31/dkc1/)). Koichi Hayashida's kishōtenketsu template fits a three-to-four-minute level neatly: introduce an idea, develop it, twist it, then conclude with a show of mastery ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-secret-to-i-mario-i-level-design)). Sands of Time built its rewind so that failure was cheap and acrobatic chains could run long ([Ubisoft News](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/3oAqiLg5k7GazIw5OWYTO2)). Reviewers of The Lost Crown said **combat and platforming "feel awkward when mixed in the same rooms."** Its best moments were one-on-one fights on flat ground ([GamingTrend](https://gamingtrend.com/feature/reviews/prince-of-persia-the-lost-crown-review-heir-to-elegance/)).

## Why the rope reads as a stick: one rod, a hard wall, no weight

**What the code does (sourced from the audit).** Each rope is a single angle and angular speed. The restoring force uses a fixed `ROPE_W2 = (2π/2.0)² ≈ 9.87 s⁻²` whatever the grip height (`index.html:1196-1199, 1276`). At the arc limit the code does `if(|ang| > 50°){ ang = ±50°; angV = 0 }`, an instant stop (`1283-1284`). The hero is not a body on the rope. He is a point placed on its line each frame, and his velocity is zeroed (`1285-1289`). The drawn rope is one `quadraticCurveTo` whose control point is offset by at most 10 px. **The visible bow is therefore about 5 px on a 220 to 300 px rope, roughly 2%**, and the tail below the hands continues in the same straight line (`2379-2395`). His painted figure is rotated by exactly the rope angle on every frame (`748`). The legs snap between two poses when the pump key changes (thigh roughly −75 to −98 while pumping, −18 to −40 when not), rather than swinging through the arc (`733-746`, `rope_log.json`). Grabbing teleports him up to 16 px onto the line with no sound, jolt or catch pose (`1242-1247`). A contact shadow is drawn under his feet in mid-air (`615-619`). Pose blending is a fixed 0.35 per rendered frame, so it runs faster on 120 Hz screens and mushier at 30 fps (`2966-2969`).

**Why that feels wrong (inference).** A real rope, and every good game rope, has three things this one lacks. It **curves**: the section above the hands bows under load and the tail below swings and lags. It **eases into the apex**: a pendulum slows naturally at the top, which gives the hang time where a release feels good. And the body **lags** behind the rope and catches up at the top. Here all three are missing, so rope, arms, torso and legs pivot as one rigid lever. The fixed period also makes the swing feel floaty when gripped high. Effective gravity at the hands is ω²·r, about **1,480 px/s² at a 150 px grip and about 99 px/s² near the top**, against **2,300 px/s² everywhere else in the game** (`1467`). A useful check: a true pendulum under the game's own 2,300 px/s² gravity has a period of 2π√(L/g), which is **about 1.9 s for a 220 px rope and 2.3 s for a 300 px rope**. The ruled 2.0 s rhythm is therefore about right *at full rope length*. The floatiness comes from letting the grip slide high on a rope that keeps that rhythm.

**The fix the evidence supports.** Use a hybrid: keep the simple pendulum for gameplay and add a bendy rope that is visual only. Contraption Maker does exactly this. A simple distance joint carries the real force, and the interior Verlet points **"transfer no force" and do not collide**. It uses **7 constraint iterations per step**, because fewer looked springy and slow to settle ([Game Developer: Ropes in Contraption Maker](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/ropes-in-contraption-maker)). Jakobsen's Hitman method is the standard recipe. Each point stores its current and previous position. Drag comes from scaling the implied velocity by about 0.99. Each stick constraint moves both ends half the correction. A point is pinned by giving it zero inverse mass, and **re-setting a point's position inside the iteration loop works as IK**, which is how Hitman dragged a body by its hand ([Game Developer: Advanced Character Physics](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/advanced-character-physics)). Applied to Chola (inference): run a 12 to 16 point chain per rope, pin the anchor, and pin the point nearest the hands to the hero's grip on every iteration. Let the points below hang free as a trailing tail, and give the section above about 1 to 3% slack so it visibly sags. Draw it as a smooth curve through the chain's midpoints ([Tutorialspoint](https://www.tutorialspoint.com/article/how-to-draw-smooth-curve-through-multiple-points-using-javascript)), tapering from about 6 px at the anchor to 3 px at the tip. Two strokes (a dark ink outline, then a lighter wash core) fit the painted look better than one flat line. A few ropes of 16 points cost almost nothing in JavaScript.

## Why the sword reads as a bunt: a straight lever, a soft ease, and nothing on contact

**What the code does (sourced from the audit).** The cut animates only the front upper arm, `lerp(-150°, 15°)`. The elbow is fixed at −20° and the torso adds 8° (`768-770`). The blade is drawn inside the forearm's transform at a fixed −0.35 rad, with no wrist joint. It is about **32 px long and 1.2 px wide on screen** (`636-643`), thin and pale against dark jungle. Because the 0.35-per-frame blend chases a moving target, **the arm never reaches its wind-up**. It peaks at about −98° on frame 2, then sweeps about 110° over about 13 frames with an even ease and no fast middle (`sword_log.json`). Legs, hips and stance do not change. A swoosh trail exists only in the unused procedural fallback figure. The painted hero returns before it is drawn (`2569-2572, 2702-2722`). On a hit, the enemy is **teleported 24 px**, flickers at 60% opacity for 0.25 s, the screen shakes 6 px for 0.15 s, and a dark ink burst appears. There is **no hitstop at all**: the main loop always advances time (`1735-1741, 3068-3079`). The shake offsets each of the three depth layers independently, so the scene jitters rather than lurches (`1889, 1926-1927`). The hitbox is a flat range test that is **live from frame 0**, before the blade comes forward, and ignores where the blade is (`1727-1731`). Attack presses during a swing are dropped, because there is no buffer (`1416-1424`). The hero has no block or parry. The fighter is still a procedural block figure, "Procedural until rig sheet 39 exists" (`2511-2522`), and his telegraph reads as a pole with a ball on top (`2528-2537`).

**The range bug.** The cut reaches 56 px past the enemy's edge, so it lands only when the two centers are within **69 px**. The fighter stops advancing at **90 px** (`FIGHT_STOP`, `1697-1700`), and his thrust reaches centers about **96 px** apart (`1669-1673`). In the headless test, a cut at 70 px missed both the fighter and the shield-bearer, and the fighter's thrust then landed. The design docs also disagree with each other. DESIGN §2 says "hitbox 52 wide, 30 px ahead," and §4 says "within 56 px ahead." The code follows §4.

**Why it looks like a bat (inference).** A bat swing is a flat sweep of a rigid lever from the shoulder, with no wrist turn. A sword cut shows a wind-up behind the shoulder, a fast middle, a wrist that changes the blade's angle against the forearm, hip and torso rotation, often a step, and a held follow-through past the target. Chola has the first silhouette and none of the second. The ease-out blend makes the swing slow at both ends, which reads as a push. The near-invisible blade means the eye follows the arm, not the weapon. **"Not clear what my sword is doing"** follows directly from three facts: no freeze on contact, a teleport instead of a knockback, and a faint blink instead of a flash. **"Not fun"** follows from the range mismatch, which produces whiffs and hits taken, and from a two-press solution with no defense to learn. The fix is the combination the sources describe: hitstop, a victim shake and flinch, a short anticipation, a smear, a held follow-through, a slightly longer blade at extension, recoil when the hero hits something, and one distinct reaction for each outcome.

The house rules shape these fixes. **No gore** means impacts are ink, cloth and metal, never blood. **Crimson is reserved for Chola**, so The Lost Crown's red "dodge this" color cannot be copied. The research team suggested vermilion, but vermilion sits too close to crimson to be safe. A better telegraph pair is **gold for "parry me"** and a **black or indigo ink seal for "get out of the way."** Hit bursts should be indigo or black ink with gold flecks, not the dark-brown puff that coins and lane shifts already use.

## Why the coins have no point: a reward look with nothing behind it

**What the code and docs say (sourced from the audit).** A pickup sets a flag and calls `spawnInk`, the same 14-dot dark puff used for lane shifts, marked swings, the handover and the shield block. It plays **no sound** (`1802-1808, 1912-1917`). The coin is drawn as a glowing, spinning, bobbing gold disc with a ring and a glyph (`2410-2423`). That is the arcade reward look, and it does not match the brief's "thick, irregular, pale gold, a standing figure, no lettering" kasu. This is deliberate in intent. VISION Pillar 3 says the game "never counts, ranks, or rewards what the player left behind," and DESIGN §6 defines kasu as a trail, "a line of three to five shows a jump arc, a swing's release, the safer lane, or the way a guard's back opens." It explicitly rules out "float, chime, HUD counter, or tally." In practice the placements often do not trace anything. Level 1 has 35 coins, level 2 has 47 and level 3 has 32, and many sit in flat rows at one height on open floor, such as `[840,380],[900,380],[960,380]` on level 1 and `[5400..6000,484]` on level 2. A few do trace arcs, such as level 1 `[1560,180]→[1720,240]` after rope 1 (`797-804, 874-880, 926-931`). Nothing in the game tells the player what the coins mean.

**Diagnosis (inference).** The visuals make a promise ("shiny reward!") and the pickup breaks it with a dark smudge that resembles a lane change or even a penalty. The intended meaning ("follow me to the better route") only works if every line clearly leads somewhere and the player is shown that once. Flat rows teach nothing. The standing rule forbids counters, chimes and tallies. It does not forbid feedback, and it does not forbid coins leading to places. DKC proves the second can work with no numbers on screen.

**What stays inside the rules, and what does not.** The following needs no rule change: redraw the kasu to the brief so it stops promising arcade points; give the pickup its own pale-gold, gold-leaf bloom and a soft non-chime sound; re-place every coin so it traces a jump, a release, a safer lane or a guard's open back; show the rule once, in level 1, with a trail that visibly leads to rope 1's release arc (the existing float-line system at `1416-1421` could carry one line). A further option, also inference, is for the remaining coins in a trail to brighten briefly when the first is touched, so the trail "lights ahead." Two stronger ideas from the references sit on the edge of the rule, and **the owner should rule on them**. The first is detour clusters that lead to a hidden vignette (a merchants' guild hall, a shrine, a scene about Srivijaya trade). A secret found could mark the voyage map the way DKC marks its levels. A map mark is not a number, but an unmarked level does point at something "left behind." The second is a few rare, named kasu that unlock short history cards. A card collection with empty slots also shows what was missed. **Kasu as currency spent at ports**, The Lost Crown's model, would need a balance shown at the point of trade. That is a counter, so it **requires the owner to relax Pillar 3**. Lives, grades and an exit quota (the DKC, AC India and Jungle Book models) all conflict with the rule or turn exploring into a chore. They should stay out.

## Prioritized changes for the builder

The order favors cheap changes that address the owner's words directly ("not fun," "not clear," "like a stick") ahead of larger rework. "Sourced" means the number or technique comes from a cited reference. "Inference" means the research team's judgment, to be tuned in play. Some ideas from the research were deliberately left off the list. Time-trial rooms and level timers, which appear in the pacing notes, would break the "no tallies of minutes" rule. A Lost Crown-style meter that fills as hits land is not a score, but it adds HUD weight the painted look may not want. Enemy health pips are defensible, since PoP 1989 used them, but diegetic signs fit the style better: a sash tearing, a weapon dropping, a fighter sagging to one knee.

## Conclusion

The common thread is that all three systems already have the right *intent* and the wrong *surface*. The rope's pump logic matches what the Krilling developers found works, and its 2-second rhythm is about right for a full-length rope. What it lacks is a visual body, an apex the swing eases into, and weight in the grab. The sword already stuns and already has a telegraphed opponent, but the player can't reach that opponent and gets no freeze on contact, so nothing registers. The coin rule is already a defensible, DKC-like idea, but the coins are drawn and placed as arcade points. Most of the fixes therefore change presentation and tuning, not systems. Items 1 to 4 can ship in an afternoon and will change how combat feels more than anything else on the list.

The less obvious implication concerns the rules themselves. The no-counter rule is compatible with collectibles that matter. DKC's best collectible layer had no on-screen number at all. But the strongest no-counter options (map marks, card collections) still show the player what was missed, so the owner will have to decide whether Pillar 3 means "no numbers" or "no reminders of what was left behind." That one ruling decides whether the kasu can ever be more than a well-drawn trail.
